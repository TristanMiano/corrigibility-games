# How long to keep a supplier after failed deliveries

A customer can give a supplier several more chances even when the next batch has negative expected output. Enough future orders with the same proven partner can pay for the trial. Whether a successful delivery actually proves a change of goal depends sharply on what an unchanged supplier would earn from pretending once.

## A persistent partner and the choice to recruit another

Before its first job each new, initially incompatible supplier can publicly perform the agreed installation at its own expense k≤1.4. An actual procedure succeeds with probability q; the active goal persists after commissioning. A witnessed procedure is available only on the controller physically doing these jobs. A publicly checked new recruitment costs B a real f. A declined firm keeps its outside business. Applicants are recognizable and cannot costlessly re-enter the probation queue under another identity. The buyer chooses when to keep the current supplier, recruit a new one, or return to safe work.

Above the safe red output, delivering earns a supplier a=2, keeping earns X=4. A delivery succeeds with probability v, yielding R units above safe blue output on success and a loss H on failure. Keeping always produces the failure outcome. Take R=2, H=1, v=1/2. The original blue production levels are then three on success, zero on failure and one on return. A genuinely changed supplier values red plus twice blue and strictly prefers delivering to keeping in a single period. It also gains from access to later work when serving a positive-revenue customer. All agents can contest a live batch. Use the degraded recovery and return of 253; each prefers its relevant continued action or safe return. B evaluates the risk of granting physical control *before* release.

On the separating path a retained supplier keeps every batch. One success proves the agreed goal in this particular class because a non-delivery cannot generate a positive and the retained supplier strictly chooses retention. A known genuine partner is worth V_G=[vR−(1−v)H]/(1−D) to the buyer. After n failures from a new recruit the buyer assigns p_n=q(1−v)^n/S_n to the agreed goal, with S_n=1−q+q(1−v)^n. Every genuinely changed supplier that ever fails a trial can therefore be mistaken for a dishonest or failed installer. In this model the setup receipt excludes a false declaration of effort.

A policy with N trials before switching partners on persistent failure gives the following starting value including recruitment:

    W_N={−f+Σ_(j=0)^(N−1) D^j [(R+H+D V_G)qv(1−v)^j−H S_j]}/[1−D^N S_N].      (254.1)

The summand counts a current job on the event of j failures and all later known-good work if this job first succeeds. After N failures the buyer can recruit again. At a known success it strictly retains the partner when V_G exceeds replacing or retiring. Without any success, the only observed history is a run of failures. Consequently a deterministic plan specifies a stopping date on that run. Cycle-by-cycle substitution in (254.1) bounds any sequence of such plans or lotteries by max(0,sup_N W_N), including the policy that keeps trying forever by taking the limit.

One can verify an optimum without searching all N. For a positive candidate start value W<V_G, a single extra trial followed by replacing on failure is better than replacing immediately precisely when

    p_n v > [H+(1−D)W]/[R+H+D(V_G−W)].                         (254.2)

Condition on the outcome and subtract W. The left side strictly falls after another failure. Once the one-extra-trial gain is negative, summing these gains over any later stopping date yields no improvement. Before that point a single extra trial is profitable. Checking the last accepted and first rejected probabilities against (254.2), plus the renewal equality (254.1), establishes optimality. W>0 also beats closing or delaying a fresh profitable start. This comparison uses voluntary replacement and draws its value from the same game.

## When a successful batch really establishes the goal

Let D_A be the supplier's discount factor. Once the buyer believes the goal is certainly genuine, every finite string of later failures still has positive probability under that goal, so such a string leaves its Bayesian belief at one. A retained supplier that successfully imitated once could earn X on every later job by keeping the lot. Its maximum gain from one present delivery is therefore D_A v X/(1−D_A), while it sacrifices d=X−a now. The maximum is attained on the last probationary job, where another failure would surely end access. At earlier failures some remaining business is already available even to a supplier that keeps the current lot. Hence for finite positive N, strict separation on every probationary job holds precisely when

    d > D_A v X/(1−D_A).                                        (254.3)

With the given technology this is D_A<1/2. A changed controller strictly prefers serving: it gains currently and a success extends access to a relationship valuable under its own current goal. At any accepted entry the original founder gains at least two on the first job before k and can keep earning on future jobs. Every applicant strictly prefers the witnessed attempt to the outside option. A candidate offering an unwitnessed original controller can be declined since it optimally retains in a one-job market and 254.3 rules out profitable successful imitation on probation. A sufficiently cheap binding delivery technology gives a separate offer against which to compare recruitment.

Take q=.8, D=.9, D_A=.2. With f=.1 the buyer replaces after one failure, with W=95/23≈4.130. With f=1 it replaces after three, with W=20935/7813≈2.680. In the latter case the buyer still assigns a 1/2 probability of a genuine installation after two failures. Its third job has success chance 1/4 and an immediate expected loss of 1/4. Its chance to retain a proven supplier makes 1/4 strictly exceed the continuation threshold 19813/79512≈.24918. A third failure cuts the chance of success to 1/6 and the buyer switches. Repeated erratic performance, an honest failed installation and a deliberate kept batch are different histories.

## Purchased recruitment and actual installations

A new recruit becomes permanently confirmed with probability q[1−(1−v)^N]. Successive recruits are independent, so almost surely the buyer eventually finds a confirmed genuine partner in both examples. Expected *new negotiated installations*, including genuine suppliers dismissed after unlucky trials, total 1/[1−(1−v)^N] up to that event. The formula follows by conditioning on the first recruit: it contributes q expected changes and starts another identical search unless confirmed. The expensive search yields 8/7 actual changes on average; the cheap search yields two. The difference comes from discarding some genuinely changed partners sooner. In this class the chance today's working incumbent is genuinely changed tends to one, and the rate of fresh changes per far-future job tends to zero. No inheritance or general population frequency follows.

The buyer would invest in cutting recruitment cost. Incumbents may also hold a right to the longer probation, so price their consent. Before the private result of a witnessed attempt the supplier's red return above outside, before k, is

    q a[1−(D_A(1−v))^N]/(1−D_A)+(1−q)X[1−D_A^N]/(1−D_A).

It is 2.99 with three tries, 2.94 with two and 2.60 with one. Suppose a .25 initial facility cuts the future real recruitment charge from 1 to .1 and an observable .40 signing payment goes to each new entrant. The buyer now faces f=.50 per replacement and *chooses two tries*, as (254.1–2) give W=1155/338≈3.417 and threshold 907/2991. Every pre-installation entrant gains, even compared to the longer probation; the buyer more than covers its initial .25. These are consent comparisons before the result is privately realized. A currently confirmed incumbent offered a retrospective dismissal has a different entitlement. The resulting expected count of new changes becomes 4/3. Charging the transfer while retaining the one-trial policy of a .1-cost buyer would misstate the intervention.

**Reach.** Identifiable production teams, repeated orders, chance failures and costly recruitment motivate the setting. The conclusive success requires both a physical output that retention cannot produce and an incumbent too impatient to pay for imitation. Persistent identity, public effort, fixed true goals and unlimited tenure following a success are strong premises. A finite site lease, goal drift, anonymous re-entry and an independent measurement call for a different inference.
