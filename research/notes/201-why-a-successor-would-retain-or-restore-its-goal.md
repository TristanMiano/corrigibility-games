# Would the successor keep a genuinely negotiated goal if restoration were possible?

**E8, note 201.** A successor that already values other sites can still profit by restoring its former goal. A visible restoration may induce a partner to offer it more, and the successor evaluates those extra receipts using its *current* concern for all affected parties. The group can answer in three different ways: pay the successor to retain its concern, negotiate stronger concern at the outset, or buy a safeguard that raises the real cost of undoing the change. These routes have different prices even though all can preserve delivery.

## A real settlement after a visible reversal

Use one exposed job from 200. Intact output x=0.70 goes to the named producer, subject to payments. Each unit of payment to an unserved site uses z=1.20 units of the recipient's productive stock. On a dispute each site obtains expected salvage q=0.20 and the initiator pays c=0.04, so d=0.16. There are m=2 unserved sites. A physical routing interface can condition payments on actual completed delivery, with sufficient capacity for m payments of d. The recipient retains its own goal. All sites can still initiate when their final independent decisions arrive.

Before any site knows its position, founders can subscribe to triggered local installations at the actual unserved sites. A configured site then evaluates its own output plus β times the other sites' terminal outputs and expenditures. After the beneficiary is named, that successor can publicly pay K=0.02 of its own useful resources to restore its former own-only criterion. The recipient can offer each incumbent a payment contingent on that site retaining its controller and on intact delivery, and then new final terms to anyone who has already restored. The last offer meets actual releases and cannot be revoked after accepted closure. Early informal statements cannot prohibit both parties from making a better new deal at the later history. Retaining a verifiable previous controller, its reinstallation downtime and the right to make a last payment are consequential implementation hypotheses. A protected earlier settlement that could rule out all later counteroffers would be another available safeguard to price.

Once a site is truly original again the recipient will pay it d to secure final acceptance (or slightly more for strict acceptance). Treat transfers previously promised to the still-concerned sites on completion as fixed at amounts ≤d each. If k restored or unmodified sites remain, each requires at least d at exposure and can collect at most q in expectation on a disputed job. Raising dispute probability by H saves at most H·kq of expected payments to those sites compared with full payment of kd. A dispute also forfeits at most the remaining (m-k)d of prior promised payments. The recipient loses at least x-q of intact value per dispute. Since x-q=0.50>zmq=0.48, buying full completion beats deliberately leaving a positive chance of contest, even allowing mixed timing and private recommendations. All still-concerned sites willingly pass regardless of the other site's restoration when

    β[x-(m-1)(z-1)d-mq] ≥ d.

This is strict at both β=0.65 and β=0.80. A still-concerned site's own payment further favors passing for these β because 1-βz>0.

## What stops a voluntary restoration?

Fix all other sites' decisions. A currently concerned site can retain its goal and take a proposed transfer t, or pay K to restore and obtain d. Under its **current** evaluator the difference in favor of restoring is

    (1-βz)(d-t)-K.                                                     (1)

It gains d-t of own receipts but values the z(d-t) taken from the payer at weight β. Other parties' completed production and the choices of other restoring sites cancel. Once restored it seeks the transfer under its genuinely own-only ranking. Equation (1) prices that later bargain before it has made the change. It demonstrates reversibility without attributing the founder's incentive to a different successor.

At β=0.65 the least credible prior transfer to retain the change is t=d-K/(1-βz)=19/275≈0.06909. The recipient willingly offers it to avoid buying off an original for 0.16. The retained original concern is genuine: if the payment were already fixed and its service came at the expense of a partner, returning to an own-only criterion would change its order over delivery and physical dispute. More precisely, with the quoted t<d the unserved site would initiate under an own-only goal if the offer were irrevocably fixed, and strictly passes under its concerned goal. At β=0.80 the gain from a free restoration-induced higher payment is at most 0.0064, already below K. The more concerned successor needs zero retaining payment.

Suppose a scoped partial installation costs 0.008 per unserved site at β=0.65 and 0.022 at β=0.80. The cheaper partial installation can optionally include an archive service that raises the real restoration cost to 0.04, by keeping the old running controller offline. The fee is 0.007 per installed site and restoration remains physically possible. The protected successor then passes without payment because its maximum restoration-induced gain of 0.0352 is below its actual cost. The ability to buy a cheap independent substitute for the backup would lower that protection and must enter any real comparison.

| Ex ante choice for one unserved controller | Cost to the founders' total original production per such site |
| --- | ---: |
| Original with a purchase of its release | (z-1)d = 0.032 |
| β=0.65, renewable negotiation and no archive | 0.008+(z-1)(19/275) ≈ 0.02182 |
| β=0.80, no archive | 0.022 |
| β=0.65 with archive | 0.015 |

The payment itself is useful to its recipient, so only the conversion loss is charged to the sum. Under a uniform prior over site identities the net material gain from the least costly arrangement is shared symmetrically in expectation. Each original founder therefore wants the guarded, genuine subscription before the winner is known. Small proposal costs can be paid from the strict margin. If the archive offer expires or costs more than 0.01382, the paid retention agreement at β=0.65 costs less than either remaining route at these quotes. If an ordinarily delegated release costs 0.010 per unserved site with equivalent physical control, the founders choose that instead. Prices near a weak acceptance boundary can be perturbed for strict assent. These are actual options in the specified sequence of offers. An upfront, unamendable cap on later payment to a restored controller at a lower price could protect genuine concern without archiving its former evaluator.

A shared total-output goal (β=1) on an already efficient intact stream has an even simpler reason to persist. If every future action and amendment merely redistributes a fixed stream of work, with nonnegative real amendment expense, no positive-cost future edit can improve its *current* total-output payoff. With scope for a new productive project or a partner able to supply output beyond the old frontier that argument changes, as in 174 and 178. In the example, an installed goal's continued choice, observed peace and preservation of the founder's former private interest are three distinct claims.

**Exploration ledger.** The inference that voluntary adoption guarantees later retention is dead when β=0.65 and there is no retaining payment or safeguard. A sibling prices the actual counteroffer a changed successor can obtain. A higher-rung successor studies how actors choose the authority to revise a protected agreement when future members can supply additional useful output.
