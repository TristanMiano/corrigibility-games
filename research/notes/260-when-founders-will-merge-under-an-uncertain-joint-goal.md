# 260 — When will two founders hand the final decision to an uncertain joint successor?

A merger ends two independent vetoes. Each founder can want the joint productive surplus and still value retaining its own agency. This is a different decision from [two surviving partners making reciprocal edits](213-both-partners-have-to-change.md). What terms buy both founders' consent when the commissioned successor may have the wrong goal?

## A single successor and two current evaluations

Red and Blue value only their own physical output. Each initially values an outcome at uᵢ(own output) plus λᵢ times an indicator that it retains meaningful independent control over its project. Their joint converter can irreversibly put one controller in charge of M units of production. Its intended goal on the **total** red and blue output (x,y) is

    Gθ(x,y) = (√(θx) + √((1−θ)y))²,    0<θ<1.

On x+y=M, Cauchy–Schwarz gives a unique maximum at (θM,(1−θ)M). This goal ranks some material lotteries differently from either founder's original goal. At the endpoints one color alone counts. Both founders consent before the converter starts. Either can still keep separate control or physically contest a refusal, with finite loss of machinery. After the irreversible switch only one residual decision maker exists on this line. Other independent parties retain their physical options. A reversible operating company with separate shareholders and residual vetoes belongs among the ordinary controls.

First take θ=1/2. An honest commissioning run installs the agreed goal with probability q, and installs each exclusive endpoint with probability (1−q)/2. The ground truth here is the ordering in the actual controller. The founders assess it before surrendering their vetoes. To insure the physical outcome, they can finish b units of each dedicated project first. Each finished unit uses 1+η units of the productive stock. Thereafter total output is Y=M−2ηb, with 0≤b≤M/[2(1+η)]. The already completed work stays with its beneficiary. The agreed controller equalizes totals. An endpoint sends all remaining production to its own color. For two founders with the same increasing concave material utility u, each values the merger at

    V(b,q) = q u(M/2−ηb) + (1−q)[u(b)+u(M−(1+2η)b)]/2.             (260.1)

They merge when this exceeds *each* founder's best available continuation including any value of retained agency. A founder with a higher independent-control premium can veto even a materially productive agreement. Heterogeneous utilities or beliefs simply give two separate participation inequalities. With fixed terms and specified future actions, these are linear in each founder's probabilities over the three possible goals.

## How much will the founders devote to protection?

For differentiable strictly concave u and η>0, V is strictly concave in b. Write m=M/2−ηb and H=M−(1+2η)b. An interior optimum equates

    [u′(b)−(1+2η)u′(H)]/2 = [q/(1−q)]ηu′(m).

At the common upper bound all three output levels coincide and Vᵦ=−ηu′(b)<0. Positive protection is optimal exactly when the right derivative of V at zero is positive. In particular, u′(0+)=∞ buys some protection for every q<1. A linear founder chooses zero because early conversion wastes material. At a positive optimum, increasing q makes the right side larger at fixed b. Strict concavity then moves the new optimum to smaller b. At any fixed b, increasing q raises expected utility by the Jensen gap of the two fault outcomes, strictly whenever their outputs differ and u is strictly concave. Better assurance about the goal and physical protection against a wrong goal can substitute for one another.

Let M=16, η=.2, q=.4, and u(x)=√x. Each founder can keep five certain units and values continuing control at .25. Safe separation is worth 2.4861. Recovery by combat leaves at most four total units in this setting and cannot beat separation even for the sure winner. The unsecured merger is worth 2.3314. Completing three units of each color first raises its value to **2.6383 for each founder**. Both now strictly sign even though the joint goal will be wrong more often than right. The optimum is about b=3.653, worth 2.6417. The probability of a faithful merger stays .4. The gain comes from protecting a project under the wrong successor.

## Which ordinary arrangements displace this merger?

Suppose an ordinary controller under two surviving vetoes can guarantee an even split of Mₒ units. Both then obtain √(Mₒ/2)+.25. The best secured merger beats it only for Mₒ<2(2.64167−.25)²≈11.44. A guaranteed seven units each with retained control wins easily. Even full preconversion of the same stock would give 6⅔ units each, worth more than the risky merger **if the founders can do it while preserving their independent control**. In that implementation both prefer the ordinary arrangement. This comparison also applies if a common delegate can generate the same output lottery and preserve the founders' valued vetoes. A delegate requiring them to surrender precisely the same control gets the same welfare score at the same price, while its installation counts as delegation.

A setting in which the merger is actually chosen needs an opportunity cost for keeping separate control. One possible source is a machine whose joint allotment is available only on irreversible transfer of both inputs to a single line. On the separate lines the owners can guarantee five each. A limited preprocessing jig can finish, for example, at most three each before surrender or separate use. Both will choose its maximum on the joint route when q=.4. A jointly enforceable split dispatching the entire allotment is a serious competing investment. The threshold above specifies the capacity it would have to supply. Which control rights the titles really convey, whether the jig can bypass the joint line, and how much it costs to add a second dispatch route are empirical premises. A simple single-pool factory with free division would instead select division. The strict consent calculation concerns these founders and these available routes. It supplies no frequency estimate for irreversible mergers.
