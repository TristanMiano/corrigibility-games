# When the original signers can form side agreements

**E8, note 193.** Changing a named founder's preferences can soften a negotiation even when every remaining group of self-interested sites can coordinate repeated disputes and share what it recovers. The price of keeping those sites willing to wait includes their ability to split *one* initiation expense. When a changed founder must be paid in actual service, there is an exact threshold at unequal strengths.

Use 191 with common intact yield one, expected individual salvages Q_i, and a common initiative expense c, with Q_i>c for all retained originals. For clarity take p=t, 0<p<1. Let O be the nonempty set of retained originals and C its nonempty complement of genuinely changed founders who need positive service. Suppose any group S of retained originals can commit to dispute together and compare the sum of its actual own-output receipts using freely transferable internal claims. Selection of a dispute leaves other agents' physical actions fixed. This is a strong outside option. It does not confer on any founder a compulsory right to govern everybody's releases. The changed sites' continuing preference for all material output makes a purely internal transfer between these same stocks neutral to their shared valuation. An outside asset which can *increase* that valuation has to be priced separately. No enforceable offers from the grand group contingent on each actual refusal are available in this comparison.

For a proposed vector z of normalized original receipts, z≥0, Σz_i=1, the exact conditions for full productive service and immunity to these retained-original groups at all future dates are

    p ≥ Q(O)-c,          Σ_(i in O) max(0,Q_i-z_i) ≤ c.                 (1)

In addition z must cover the actual reservation-plus-setup of each founder before the vote. Strict inequalities with sufficient slack allow strict consent and strict stand-down, by designing the docket against a slightly lower c. Groups with cheap means of signing side agreements, a scarce sequential booking and named participants motivate this test. The ability to transfer returns within any such group is deliberately generous and should be measured for a real project.

**Necessity.** A group S can secure Q(S)-c on every booking, even when its most convenient member acts while the others wait. The expected next-job material return to all originals is at most one. On a booking actually paying a changed founder, O receives zero now, so its security requires p≥Q(O)-c. At the initial lottery every S⊆O also requires z(S)≥Q(S)-c. Maximizing Q(S)-z(S) over subsets amounts to including exactly the positive deficits. This gives the second inequality. A deficient group could improve its aggregate income and use internal transfers, which is why restricting a proof to separate individual approvals misses part of the bill.

**Sufficiency.** Write a_i=0 for changed founders. Distribute the single expense c among the retained originals in numbers e_i≥max(0,Q_i-z_i), with e_i≤Q_i and Σe_i=c. Condition (1) allows this since Q(O)>c. Put a_i=Q_i-e_i for each retained site. For every group S⊆O, a(S)≥Q(S)-c. Now use 191's public states v^j=a+(1-Σa)e_j, where e_j here denotes the jth unit vector. Because Σa=Q(O)-c≤p and z≥a, all transition probabilities and the initial division are feasible. On a deviation the same public state is repeated next time. The largest current dispute return to S net of its one initiative is Q(S)-c, so a group of any size prefers its prescribed current-plus-future holding v^j(S). Iterating the one-decision inequality and using bounded values excludes profitable repeated disputes by that fixed group. Changed founders keep their preference for efficient service; every site retains physical initiative at relevant live histories. When p and t differ, 189's factor (1-p)/(1-t) scales both Q and c in (1). Conditional survival after a compliant booking remains p.

Applying this to 192, changing the strongest leaves the two other originals with combined claim 0.24+0.12-0.05=0.31. Changing the two weaker ones leaves the strongest's claim 0.39-0.05=0.34. Both fit within p=0.35. In either full-service offer, giving any retained originals at least the benchmark R_i=Q_i-0.05/3 leaves their sum of deficits at most 2(0.05/3)<0.05. Thus both winning configurations have calendars robust to those repeated side disputes, and strictly positive net returns under their old goals can pay all signers. This is an execution check conditional on the quoted fallback. Permitting every coalition to rewrite that fallback in the preliminary negotiation would require valuing the new competing offers.

For distinct initiator costs c_i, the necessary condition on any promised vector becomes (Q_i-z_i)+Σ_(j in O, j≠i)(Q_j-z_j)_+≤c_i for each possible initiator i. It suffices to let i recruit every other site with a positive deficit. The already-proved sufficient construction can conservatively charge the common minimum cost. A site with an especially cheap initiative can receive more than its own salvage to keep it from joining a group. The strongest necessary bound and that conservative construction need not describe the same set of initial divisions.

**Exploration ledger.** The framing candidate asks what a whole group could actually negotiate if it declined. The structural comparison converts a fixed group's return to an explicit budget of protected future receipts. The assertion that the sum of separate initiative expenses prices the group is dead when several recoveries need only one initiative. A sibling allocates that one cost across members. A higher-rung successor tests overlapping memberships, heterogeneous participation technology and proposals from multiple groups whose outside behavior changes after a refusal. Related treatments explicitly model recursive coalition structures and responses to blocking. This note uses a fixed outsider policy.
