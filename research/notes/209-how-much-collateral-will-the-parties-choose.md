# How much collateral will the parties choose?

A partner uncertain about a goal can insure the exposed batch. Insurance can encourage a genuine change by making it harder for an unchanged controller to profit from the handover. Stronger insurance can also remove the customer's reason to verify. This produces a priceable choice about how much evidence and security to demand.

## A real alternative to buying certainty

Use the balanced-strength factory game in [206](206-when-a-truthful-attempt-can-fail.md). A physical meter can distinguish delivered from retained material at the end of the processing slot. It cannot read a terminal preference. A supplier can lodge at most L units of transferable reserve, returned unless the supplier receives the batch and keeps it. On retention the meter routes f units to B, with 0≤f≤L. The reserve is outside the contested batch and the same after-return and combat values apply. Its use consumes λf units of B's output in rental for the offer; the principal is an actual transfer, not material destroyed by default. A cannot recover it by attacking after payment. This last custody and timing assumption requires an actual payment technology.

With 0≤f<2 an original A still keeps the batch, now getting 6−f while B gets f. The negotiated half-concern still delivers: its delivery score is 7, its retention score 6−f/2. For any f>2 an original A also strictly delivers, subject to the named actions' capacity and attack comparisons. At f=2 it is indifferent. Thus a sufficiently capacious ordinary bond can implement delivery while the supplier retains its original ordering. Downstream action observability can be much cheaper than a semantic test, but still has to be feasible.

## A small bond raises adoption, a larger one weakens verification

Before the ordinary threshold, A's baseline under test is 2 and its gain from a false pass is X−f=4−f. The genuine success branch earns the original A 4 on delivery. A privately costly attempt, including its possible honest failure, gives precisely the cutoff in 206 with Z_Q(f)=1.4+.1f. For B, g=4, h=2−f. Its highest-installation equilibrium therefore uses

    π_Q(f)=(112+8f)/175,       π_U(f)=(17−9f)/(22−9f).

Within the active interval the installation rate is the smaller of these quantities by 207. The first increases because a fake can obtain less, while the second decreases because declining a retained controller protects less of B's output. The unique crossing on [0,1] is

    f*=(743−√404881)/144, about .74.                              (209.1)

Cross-multiplication gives 72f²−743f+511=0. The active inspection interval stays nonempty throughout [0,1]. After f*, B mixes trust and testing at the indifference value π_U. If capacity extends farther, the inspection interval disappears at f=58/31, where its maximum gross value equals the .1 bill. For 58/31<f<2 the original still keeps, B prefers declining a *known* retained supplier, and an attempt gives no incentive advantage without a credible inspection. The stated menu's only trade rate is then zero. Above 2, actual delivery is ordinary and enforceable. The two regimes have different physical and preference outcomes.

## Paying to choose the bond

Give B authority to offer f in [0,1] before A privately decides what to do, at λ=.01. Consider the upper equilibrium branch at each publicly posted f. All prospective suppliers strictly accept the offer compared with their fallback because even a false signer gains a positive expected amount. Before f*, B's incremental payoff is

    π_Q t g − (1−π_Q)e h − c − λ f.

Its derivative is π'_Q(tg+eh)+e(1−π_Q)−λ, strictly positive here. After f*, untested trust and testing tie. Before the rental, B's gain becomes

    [g h (t−e)−c(g+h)] / [(1−e)h+(1−t)g].

Its derivative with respect to h is g(t−e)[(1−t)g+c] divided by the squared denominator. It strictly falls as more collateral reduces h, and the rental also rises. Thus B strictly chooses f* within its physical capacity. The chosen interior bond raises actual installations above .64, while B's option to waive testing in response to stronger protection prevents a blanket preference for maximum collateral. All post-signal releases, default choices and final conflict options use the changed payoffs.

If A can insist on the former f=0 offer, A can lose some rent by consenting. On [0,f*] both endpoints use the same pure-test rule. Holding its prior action fixed, each supplier loses at most ef compared with f=0. Honest failures bear the bond on only (1−q)e of attempts; deliberate false signers bear it on e. Reoptimization can only improve these bounds. B can fund a signing payment slightly above ef. Its pre-payment gain per extra unit of f is at least (8/175)tg−λ>.15, exceeding e=.1. A funded payment made before the private action reaches actual types, including those who can refuse or later prove unable to install. The payment is common across attempt and fabrication and is sunk at the remaining decision nodes. Under these one-batch terms both founders strictly prefer the repair to their former agreement. The high equilibrium continuation is an assumption about selection. In the mixed-decline assessment B earns its fallback before the rental, giving it a different buying incentive.

If both can access a meter with L>2 at the same rental rate, an ordinary bond just above two yields B nearly 6−.02 and A 4. These exceed the balanced-branch values in the restricted capacity-one game, even for A's cheapest sincere cost type (its expected gross own return there is below four). A binding conventional delegate that can ensure delivery at a lower cost can deliver the same ordinary improvement. More collateral can be costly to finance when assets are tied up, and final material delivery can be hard to measure. There is no presumption that the high-capacity facility is available at that price.

**Naturality and reach.** Refundable deposits, a meter for output and a real limit on available collateral are individually motivated in a batch transaction. The test interface for a terminal goal remains more demanding. In this model the customer pays rental, low-cost installers pay to change, and all signers can refuse. The comparative shift at f* is produced by the buyer's best response, and the signing payment shows how a supplier with another offer can consent. A fresh withdrawal window, cross-batch liabilities, mistaken delivery measurements and a renegotiable meter are separate feasibility questions. A sibling tests measurement error in the output meter. A higher-level successor asks which dimensions of a bargain should be secured by output rights and which by evidence about successor preferences.
