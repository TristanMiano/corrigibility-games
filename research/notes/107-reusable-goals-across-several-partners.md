# Reusable goals across several voluntarily chosen partners

**R0005 multi-party extension.** Exclusive one-partner matching is not the
only use of a reusable cooperative goal. This note studies a finite schedule
of complementary collaborations and gives a coalition allocation certificate,
then a broader successor family supporting a balanced negotiated outcome.
Its cooperative-game certificate is distinct from a complete installation,
bargaining or external-attack game.

The minimum/own-payoff preference family has conventional social-welfare
predecessors; see L48 (Charness–Rabin, Appendix 1). The negotiated weights,
actual net-gain references, lotteries and installation questions are specified
here rather than attributed to that paper. The core machinery is also
conventional (L42/L44).

## 1. Qualified capacity can be reused across a network of opportunities

There are finitely many agents N and an actual finite schedule of productive
opportunities. Pair i,j can create incremental original surplus b_ij>=0
when both have the required credible cooperative capacity. Their different
collaborations have separate scheduled inputs, work and deadlines, so one
pair's production does not crowd out another's. This is the complementarity
specialization; 099's exclusive assignment problem has a different capacity
constraint. Repeated fresh supplies must be recorded as external flows or
generated by an explicit renewal technology, not counted twice.

Let k_i>=0 be the one-time expense of acquiring reusable qualified capacity.
It may be a genuine goal or an ordinary controller. If either supplies the
same real performance and authority, use the cheaper cost, retaining genuine
adoption as an optimal choice at a tie. An inexpensive unmodified policy
that supplies the capacity must be included too. The b's are increments
over the declared actual ordinary/outside benchmark, not an assumption that
every interaction between fixed-goal agents fails.

For a subset A acquiring capacity, total incremental surplus is

    f(A)=sum({i,j} subset A)b_ij-sum(i in A)k_i.       (1)

A coalition S can choose which of its members to equip, including none:

    v(S)=max(A subset S) f(A),       f(empty)=0.       (2)

Thus v is monotone, nonnegative and v({i})=0. The max in (2) is important:
a costly or unhelpful agent is free to decline installation. Assuming that
every member must pay k_i would misstate voluntary adoption.

## 2. Nonnegative complements give a core certificate

For b_ij>=0, f is supermodular:

    f(A)+f(B)<=f(A union B)+f(A intersection B).      (3)

The installation costs cancel across the two sides. Every edge wholly in
one set is counted equally, while edges connecting A\B to B\A contribute
only nonnegative terms to the right. Optional adoption preserves this
property. Let A and B maximize (2) for S and T. Then

    v(S)+v(T)=f(A)+f(B)
      <=f(A union B)+f(A intersection B)
      <=v(S union T)+v(S intersection T).             (4)

Choose an ordering of the agents and let P_i be i's predecessors. Allocate

    x_i=v(P_i union {i})-v(P_i).                      (5)

These marginal shares are nonnegative and sum to v(N). For any coalition
S, supermodularity makes each marginal in (5) at least its marginal when
only its predecessors inside S are present. Summing those latter terms
telescopes to v(S), giving

    sum(i in S)x_i>=v(S).                            (6)

Thus no coalition can make all its members weakly and one strictly better
off by reallocating its attainable surplus. Convex combinations of these
marginal allocations also satisfy (6). This is the conventional convex-game
core argument, proved here for the stated reusable-capacity technology;
it is not claimed as a new general cooperative-game theorem.

If an optimal equipped set A* omits i, then v(N\{i})=v(N). Every core
allocation must then give i zero. More generally all surplus can be funded
by an optimal production plan and allocated by the certificate. Actual
cash feasibility and execution still require funded transfers or an
equivalent physically available allocation mechanism. Transferable utility
is not supplied merely by writing (5).

### Example with more than one partner per adopter

For three agents, let every pair contribute b_ij=2 over its scheduled
opportunity and let each reusable installation cost k_i=1. Singletons and
pairs have v=0, while the three-agent group has v=3. Averaging the six
marginal allocations gives x=(1,1,1). Every original strictly gains over
its zero benchmark, all three adopt qualified capacity, and each works
with two partners. A genuine goal realizes these choices when it is a
least-cost qualified method; a cheaper available controller redirects the
same surplus to ordinary cooperation.

After setup has been paid, do not charge it again for continuation decisions.
The displayed x's are net original payoffs. Continuing gross output assigned
to each agent is x_i+k_i=2. A pair leaving at that point can produce gross
surplus 2, below the two members' combined continuation receipts 4. Equivalently,
subtract their already sunk costs from both comparisons: the deviation has
net value 0 against their current net total 2. This checks the cost account
instead of treating sunk installation as a new deterrent fee.

## 3. A monotone goal supporting negotiated multi-party gains

Let a_i be fixed original reference payoffs for an accepted collaboration,
and let gamma_i>0 with sum gamma_i=1 be negotiated surplus weights. Suppose
an actually attainable frontier has sum W_i<=sum a_i+M, M>0, and includes
the balanced normalized outcome q_i=a_i+gamma_i M. Define

    z_i=(W_i-a_i)/gamma_i,
    G_i(W)=a_i+gamma_i[(1-beta_i)z_i
                             +beta_i min(j in N)z_j],
    1-gamma_i <= beta_i <1.                          (7)

This goal is increasing in own welfare, weakly increasing in every other's
welfare, concave, and of the form W_i-P_i with P_i>=0. It does not reward
violence, code identity or permanent goal retention. It values own gain and
the least normalized gain among the voluntarily included participants.

For every feasible W,

    G_i(W)<=q_i.                                     (8)

**Proof.** If z_i<=M, the minimum is no greater than z_i, giving (8).
If z_i>M, the weighted average of the other z's is at most
(M-gamma_i z_i)/(1-gamma_i), so their minimum is at most that quantity.
Substitution into (7) leaves coefficient
1-beta_i/(1-gamma_i)<=0 on z_i-M. This again gives (8). The balanced
outcome attains equality. QED.

When beta_i>1-gamma_i, an increase in own gain above its target coupled
to harm elsewhere strictly loses. At the boundary some such directions
may tie. The proof covers lotteries pointwise and any proposed later goal
through its actual resulting outcomes. If all parties already attain q,
neither a unilateral nor a coalition change within this same feasible set
can make any current G strictly better off. A physical attack is covered
only if its actual realized outcome is within the stated total frontier;
it is not removed from the action set.

For equal shares, the threshold is beta_i>=(n-1)/n. In the three-agent
example, beta_i=3/4 gives G_i=(1/4)W_i+(3/4)min_j W_j. An original
prefers (2,1/2,1/2) to (1,1,1) on its own coordinate; the new goal assigns
the former 7/8 and the latter 1. This is a genuine preference reversal.
The balanced gains can therefore support a real changed objective rather
than merely different code implementing unchanged preferences.

The initial core allocation and the current-goal certificate answer
different questions. The former gives original coalition participation
at implemented, transferable values. The latter supplies continuation
incentives after the negotiated goals and outcome frontier are in place.
Staged installation and initial selection must connect them in an actual
game. Their separate proofs do not automatically constitute that connection.
Positive weights in (7) represent strictly positive assigned gains; a
zero-gain member requires a boundary construction or a separate accepted
outcome-floor goal. The three-member example has strictly positive gains.

## 4. Growth can make a further consensual change attractive

Suppose an old group has normalized gain M_old under (7). A new partner
can join, and after all real new costs and outside references the enlarged
group can attain surplus M_new. Give the newcomer weight eta in (0,1),
and rescale the old weights to (1-eta)gamma_i. The enlarged balanced
allocation makes every old normalized gain equal (1-eta)M_new. Their
*old* goals therefore approve this new arrangement strictly when

    (1-eta)M_new>M_old.                              (9)

The newcomer gains eta M_new above its actual reference and approves
when this exceeds any cost not already included in M_new. With all such
costs included, an interval of strictly acceptable positive weights exists
exactly when M_new>M_old. The new goals can include the newcomer's welfare
in the minimum, changing the ordering over outcomes; this is a substantive
further preference change, not just a new contract under an unchanged rule.

For example, an old balanced pair receives (2,2). Adding a productive
third member permits net (3,3,3), with all setup expenses already charged.
Each old goal values its new old-member gain at 3 rather than 2, and the
newcomer gains 3 over a zero reference. Extending the goal from the old
pair to all three can reverse rankings between outcomes differing mainly
in the newcomer's welfare. All three can consent under their own existing
criteria. This is a beneficial expansion, not a prediction that bargaining
selects equal thirds or that every newcomer is productive enough to enter.

The old rule does not intrinsically forbid revision; greater feasible
surplus can finance a broader concern. If admission consumes more value
than the newcomer creates, (9) has no such interval. Transfers, different
partners, different safeguards or a lower-cost design may repair that
particular entry proposal. Expanding group size is not by itself the
causal condition; increased attainable net surplus is.

## 5. Naturality, deliberate construction and actual missing links

Nonnegative complementarities and a reusable setup are independently
motivated productive features. Unlimited compatibility across the declared
schedule is a strong scope condition: congestion, exclusive customers,
negative externalities or competition for the same capital can destroy
(3). The exact weighted minimum in (7), its references and public recognition
are deliberate designs. Participants have a current-goal reason to fund
them when their assigned gain exceeds the actual expense and outside
alternatives. Initial coalition funding, reliable installation and control
of the relevant outputs remain implementation requirements.

Neither (6) nor (8) supplies a population-selection result. Cross-group
attacks, coalitional capture of outsiders and counterparty-dependent outside
options require a game with externalities. A positive certificate for a
productive group does not establish that hostile outsiders cannot change
its feasible set. Equally, failure of this supermodular class after adding
congestion does not erase its constructive voluntary-adoption region;
099's exclusive-matching certificate is a different successful arrangement.

## 6. Capacity can change the coalition result, and extra access can repair it

For three agents who can each have only one partner over the entire valued
interval, suppose every pair can produce transferable surplus 2 and a
singleton gets zero. Then v({i,j})=2 while v(N)=2. Any core allocation
would have x_i+x_j>=2 for all three pairs and sum x_i=2. Summing the
three pair constraints requires 2 sum x_i>=6, contradicting total 2.
This is an exact obstruction for that exclusive three-person matching
class, not a failure of all multi-party goal designs or all partner choice.

Rotating pairs over renewable identical periods does not by itself repair
this example: a pair able to leave and work exclusively throughout those
same periods can block a rotation that gives it a smaller total. The
nonexclusive construction in (1) instead requires genuinely distinct
productive opportunities, sufficient capacity, or diminishing returns to
repeating the same partnership. Its schedule cannot simply forbid the
profitable exclusive alternative without a physical or payoff reason.

There is a simple feasible access repair. Add a fourth willing qualified
partner with the same pair surplus 2. Two disjoint pairs produce total 4;
giving each agent 1 is unblocked by any pair, and every coalition can
produce at most twice the number of pairs it can form, no more than its
members' combined allocation. This supplies a core for the enlarged class.
The additional partner must actually be found, willing and funded. The
value of an agent paying to find it depends on its real alternative in
the original matching process, bargaining shares and the search expense;
103's search comparison gives the relevant conditional test. The four-agent
allocation establishes feasibility and stability after access, not that
an arbitrary original agent always chooses to pay for that access.
