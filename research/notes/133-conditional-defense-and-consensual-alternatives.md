# Conditional defensive goals and consensual alternatives

R0006 intervention extension. This separates unilateral self-modification
that creates a defensive incentive from consensual changes that can replace
it. The same exclusive original color values support either, depending on
feasible implementation expenses and timing. A positive self-modification
finding is not automatically evidence of consensual accommodation.

## 1. A credible defense must have an acting incentive

Initially each party has native production capacity R, fully cross-usable
after a fight. Originals value their own final color wherever produced.
Blue wins with probability p, and finite damage/recovery leaves fraction
delta in (0,1) usable before the deadline. Assume p delta>1/2. Without
protection, Blue can threaten a fight whose expected outcomes are

`d_B=2p delta R`, `d_R=2(1-p)delta R`.

With Blue's offer right and an affordable ordinary execution route, Red's
accepted value is d_R; if the specified agreement menu cannot fund a deal,
the same d_R is its combat expectation. Compare all other real options
before using this as the original benchmark.

Suppose that after observing a Blue-initiated attack, but before battle
resolves, Red can physically render fraction s of its own native stock
unusable, s in [0,1]. The action is fast enough, funded, observable and
cannot be stopped by the already launched attack. Let the affected stock
already include any finite activation work required. Its post-action
recoverable amount is R(1-s). This is a specific defensive-control
technology, not automatic agency after defeat. Combat still resolves
probabilistically; Red need not win to carry out the earlier action.

An unchanged original Red strictly prefers s=0 after the attack: its
expected own output is `(1-p)delta(2R-Rs)`. Thus a merely verbal threat
to destroy the stock is not credible in this game. A committed automatic
device is one possible ordinary repair, with an actual cost below.

## 2. A genuine conditional preference can make the action credible

Before Blue can launch, Red can pay funded installation cost J and choose
the fixed terminal-history utility

`G_R(Hist)=q_R-beta 1{Blue initiated an attack} q_B`,
`beta>(1-p)/p`.                                        (1)

The relevant physical attack event is reliably attributable; Red cannot
create it by renaming its own attack. At histories with no Blue attack,
the current criterion remains the own-color ordering. After the event,
the coefficient on s in expected G_R is

`delta (R-J)[beta p-(1-p)] >0`.                         (2)

It therefore chooses complete disabling, s=1, when attacked. The remaining
combat stock is Blue's R, so Blue's original attack payoff is p delta R<R.
Blue instead chooses native production. In the cost region (3), Red's current peaceful choice also
remains native production: initiating a fight itself does not trigger the
punitive branch, and its original expected combat output is smaller.

The change is genuine: after attack, original Red prefers retaining the
stock, while G_R strictly prefers disabling it. It is a changed ordering
of terminal histories/lotteries, not a claim that a promise or a code label
binds the old preference. Once attacked, later goal revisions are evaluated
using current G_R and cannot improve on the specified optimal disabling
action within this continuation set. Reliable implementation and the time
to act remain prerequisites.

Initial original Red obtains R-J and strictly prefers this defensive change
to the unprotected threat continuation exactly when

`J < R[1-2(1-p)delta]`.                                (3)

This assumes no better available mechanism; a cheaper automatic device
with the same physical trigger and authority wins instead, and equal costs
can tie. An actual conditional device may be easier to build than a semantic
goal change, or the reverse; the labels do not establish a cost ordering.
The physical ability to disable assets must be given to both comparisons.

The induced outcome is peaceful native production and **one unilateral
genuine goal change**. It is not consensual concern for the rival's clips.
It illustrates a deliberately created incentive that makes aggression
unprofitable, while also showing why counting all utility changes as
cooperative adoption would misstate the finding.

## 3. A consensual goal can save the expense of defensive commitment

Now give the parties an agreement opportunity *before* Red pays J. On
rejection, Red can make the feasible defensive change above and Blue then
keeps native output. The credible rejection values are consequently

`(R, R-J)`.

Suppose a reliably installed negotiated common goal costs K_B and K_R
units of native work, with the full staged interface and total-control
audit actually available. Its funded output simplex has total

`P=2R-K_B-K_R`.

A split q* strictly improving both rejection values exists exactly when

`K_B+K_R < J`.                                         (4)

The admissible Blue share is `R<q_B*<R+J-K_B-K_R`, with
q_R*=P-q_B*. Both initially exclusive originals consent using their own
counts. On a finite offer grid with rejection at indifference, Red optimally
offers the smallest Blue share strictly above R, and Blue accepts, provided
that grid point still gives Red more than R-J. With acceptance at equality,
the optimal offer can instead give Blue R and Red the remaining surplus;
Blue's adoption then ties its rejection option. Compare better outside
routes if they exist. The savings come from avoiding a real defensive installation,
not from awarding an agent newly invented utility.

For a concrete share-selecting genuine goal use

`G(q_B,q_R)=q_B^alpha q_R^(1-alpha)`,
`alpha=q_B*/P in (0,1)`.                               (5)

Over q_B+q_R<=P its unique output maximum is q*. Differentiate the
one-dimensional positive frontier, or maximize its logarithm there, to
obtain the ratio q_B/q_R=alpha/(1-alpha). The actual utility is the
finite product (5), with value zero at either zero-color boundary; no
infinite penalty or physically deleted action is introduced. Expected
utility is E[G(q)], not G of expected counts.

After both have this goal, its global maximum rules out profitable attack
or goal revision within the same paid-production set. A fight lowers the
available total through finite damage and recovery, so even its best color
allocation gives a strictly smaller maximum. The modified winner may
produce both colors; scoring it as an original all-own-color winner would
miscompute the continuation. Partial-delivery attack checks use the actual
remaining resource bound, for example note 129's paced implementation.

If each party controls a divisible part of the productive budget and can
produce either color, the common-goal production game also has the unique
aggregate outcome q*. At any equilibrium both aggregate colors are positive
(an agent with a positive budget could otherwise make both and improve G).
On that interior G is differentiable and concave. Adding the coordinate
optimality inequalities makes the aggregate allocation globally optimal.
Monotonicity uses all available work, and the unique frontier optimum
then fixes the aggregate split, though who manufactures each color can vary.
Actual raw inputs, converters and throughput must support those actions.

For R=5, p=4/5, delta=99/100, a defense cost J=1 satisfies (3).
If each genuine installation costs 1/4, the consensual plan has P=9.5
and can give Blue 5.25 and Red 4.25, both above the defensive rejection
values (5,4). On a quarter-clip offer grid with Blue rejecting equality,
this is Red's best accepted offer. Here alpha=21/38. Those are net physical
clip counts.

## 4. Controls, timing and remaining obligations

An ordinary enforceable allocation costing H_o achieves the same split
opportunity when H_o<J. If it is cheaper than K_B+K_R and available with
matched authority, it can displace the common goal. At equal cost both
routes can be selected. If the genuine route is a least-cost available
implementation satisfying (4), this is an actual consensual alternative
to the defensive modification. A route's availability includes the entire
installation path, not just the existence of (5). Notes 128 and 131 give
specific conditional interfaces with distinct scope; neither supplies a
free atomic bilateral update.

Once J has already been paid, it is sunk; a later bargain cannot count
its avoidance as fresh surplus. Earlier bargaining is therefore material.
Faster attacks, hidden disabling capability, asset recovery that bypasses
the safeguard, or another valuable partner change the rejection values.
If Red can destroy the rival's assets without an attributable attack, (1)
needs a new incentive check. These are meaningful feasible-repair and
failure directions, not reasons to erase the supported construction.

The initial deterrence calculation gives Blue an original production/attack
choice after the installation. A later public round in which Blue installs
a deliberately aggressive controller and then renegotiates is a larger
game. It can exploit the very low threatened value of the uncalibrated (1).
The following construction tests that counter-design rather than promoting
the first deterrence result to arbitrary goal menus.

## 5. A calibrated reservation against a later aggressive commitment

Keep the actual finite native stocks, battle probability and recovery
technology fixed during a later counteroffer round. Red can instead install

`G_R^cal=q_R+1{Blue initiated attack}(M-beta q_B)`,
`M=R-J-epsilon-delta R[(1-p)-beta p]`,                  (6)

where beta>(1-p)/p and
`0<epsilon<R-J-2(1-p)delta R`. All trigger/calibration work belongs in J.
The history-dependent constant changes the comparison between peaceful
histories and attack histories; it is not a common affine change of the
old goal. It leaves the after-attack disabling incentive (2) unchanged.
After complete disabling, Red's current expected attack value is now

`V_def=R-J-epsilon`.                                   (7)

Blue's original all-own-color production is the worst post-victory allocation
for this current Red goal: every replacement of Blue clips by Red clips
raises G_R^cal. Therefore Red can secure at least V_def by disabling its
stock even against another Blue production goal, within the fixed physical
continuation. If Blue instead passes after rejection, Red keeps R-J.
Red-initiated combat does not trigger the bonus and is worse than V_def
under the stated epsilon bound. Thus preemptive attack is not needed to
escape the threatened conditional penalty.

Any accepted counteroffer that avoids a Blue attack has current value equal
to its actual q_R. The available rejection continuation proves the bound

`accepted peaceful q_R >= R-J-epsilon`.                 (8)

This holds even if Blue's offer is backed by an actually installed
aggressive controller; the original-calibration goal evaluates that full
continuation before consenting to another change. The current-original
gain relative to the unprotected benchmark is protected whenever J+epsilon
satisfies (3). The bound concerns accepted peaceful offers. It does not
by itself deter every publicly chosen combat-production controller: such
a controller can promise some Red clips after victory and change Red's
optimal disabling action. Section 6 derives the additional condition needed
to cover that deviation. Mixed threats and additional resource/strength
investments need their actual continuation analysis.

This is a deliberately calibrated current-goal safeguard, analogous in
purpose to note 128's entry reservation. It protects accepted peaceful
original output; it is not a statewise original-welfare guarantee if a
surprise attack actually occurs. After attack the successor's current
criterion and the founder's actual red clips differ sharply. A changed
attack probability or accessible stock changes (7) and must be reassessed;
accurate physical calibration is a real information requirement.

Conditional punishment and its calibration are deliberately designed goals with demanding
trigger semantics. Finite costs, reusable/capturable assets and physical
disabling mechanisms have independent motivations. The chosen defense and
the cheaper consensual replacement are constructive parameter regions;
their prevalence and actual implementation costs remain unmeasured.

## 6. Post-victory concessions and a stronger defensive condition

Keep this phase's physical stocks and p,delta fixed. After observing a
Blue production controller, Red can choose the remaining pre-recovery
stock `S in [R,2R-J]` by disabling its own stock. Let B(S) and C(S) be
the Blue and Red counts the controller would produce if Blue wins, with
`B(S)+C(S)<=delta S`. If Red wins, its current attacked criterion makes
all-Red production optimal. Blue's founder payoff from the fight is
`P_B(S)=p B(S)`; Red's current expected value obeys

`G_R^cal(S)=M+(1-p)delta S+p[C(S)-beta B(S)]`
`          <=M+delta S-(1+beta)P_B(S)`.                 (9)

Red can always fully disable, leaving S=R and guaranteeing at least
`V_def=M+delta R-(1+beta)p delta R`, whatever the Blue controller.
At any selected optimal disabling action S*, combining that guarantee
with (9) gives

`P_B(S*) <= p delta R+delta(S*-R)/(1+beta)`
`         <= p delta R+delta(R-J)/(1+beta)`.             (10)

Hence the following additional finite condition makes every such
deterministic public combat-production controller weakly inferior to
Blue's native R, before its own extra expense:

`beta >= delta(R-J)/[R(1-p delta)]-1`.                  (11)

Together with beta>(1-p)/p, a strict version of (11) supplies a stronger
deterrent. The calibration M is adjusted by (6) so the defender's current
attack reservation remains R-J-epsilon. The original Red still evaluates
paying J by its peaceful R-J. This repair changes an actual chosen goal,
not the physical destruction rate, and remains dependent on reliable
installation and fixed-stock calibration.

The bound is nearly sharp for the controller class. Choose, for a small
eta>0,

`p B(S)=p delta R+[delta/(1+beta)-eta](S-R)`,
`C(S)=delta S-B(S)`.                                   (12)

For `0<eta<delta/(1+beta)` and beta>(1-p)/p these are feasible nonnegative
counts throughout the interval. Equation (9) then holds with equality,
and Red's current value increases strictly with S. It chooses no disabling,
S=2R-J. Original Blue approaches the upper bound (10) as eta tends to zero.
Thus committing to concessions *conditional on the preserved stock* can
profitably evade the earlier, weaker deterrent when (11) fails. The
controller must actually enforce this production policy; a revocable
original promise would produce all Blue after winning and restore the
old disabling incentive.

For R=5, p=4/5, delta=99/100, J=1, beta=1, choose eta=1/100.
At S=9, (12) gives `P_B=59/10=5.9>5`,
`B=59/8=7.375`, `C=307/200=1.535`. Total output is 8.91.
Red's current value strictly favors preserving its stock. Its founder
expected Red count is `301/100=3.01`, below the peaceful 4; current and
original evaluations differ. This need not rely on zero implementation
expense. Pay 1/10 from Blue's native stock to implement (12), leaving
`r_B=49/10`; replace R by r_B in that controller's lower stock endpoint.
With Red's remaining stock still four, the attack gives original Blue
`(4/5)(99/100)(49/10)+(99/200-1/100)4=3638/625=5.8208`,
above its original no-installation native five. Red's old calibration M
is kept fixed; it adds a constant to its disabling comparison and does not
remove its strict incentive to preserve the stock. This is a funded
unilateral controller choice leading to combat, not consensual adoption.
In the same fixed-stock comparison,
choosing beta=3 satisfies (11), whose threshold is 73/26, and bounds Blue's
combat payoff by `99/20=4.95<5`. Genuine defensive redesign therefore
repairs this particular richer commitment attack at finite parameter values.

This closes a specific gap in the counteroffer claim. It covers arbitrary
deterministic feasible victory allocations indexed by S, not additional
weapon investments, changes in battle probabilities, new destruction
rights or every possible later bargaining process. A matched conditional
automatic controller can implement either strategy at its actual cost.
Consensual agreements that save J remain constructive alternatives, but
their credible defensive fallback must use the strengthened continuation
when this richer combat-controller menu is actually available.
