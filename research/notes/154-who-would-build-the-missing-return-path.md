# Who would build the missing link in a circle of reciprocal favors?

**E8, note 154.** Three founders can each gain from a new service connection while disagreeing about whether to pay for it. The decisive question is which extra future referrals the other sites can credibly offer the builder. In the worked example either endpoint could construct a connection with exactly the same technology. One can recover the construction expense under a negotiated goal; the other cannot.

## The maximum expense a circle can carry

Specialize [153](153-when-reciprocal-referrals-can-pay-all-founders.md) to a directed ring of m≥2 members. Edge i runs from i to i+1, indices modulo m. Its expected donor cost capacity is c_i>0 and its recipient-to-donor original gain ratio is g_i>0. Put G=Π_i g_i and P_ij=Π_{h=0}^{j-1}g_(i+h), so P_im=G. Member i requires at least k_i≥0 in net original utility to cover its real setup and available outside option. Choose an additional common margin s≥0. If G>1 the smallest feasible edge flows for those target gains are

    x_i(s) = [Σ_{j=1}^m (k_(i+j)+s)/P_ij]/(1-1/G).                (1)

Indeed the requirements g_i x_i-x_(i+1)≥k_(i+1)+s can be rewritten as lower bounds on x_i. Substituting them consecutively around the ring gives (1) as a lower bound. Equality in all m requirements solves the same recurrence, so the vector of lower bounds itself supplies the target. A strict improvement for everyone exists iff x_i(0)<c_i for every i (with G>1). The largest achievable nonnegative common margin, when such margins are feasible, is the smallest over i of

    [(1-1/G)c_i - Σ_j k_(i+j)/P_ij] / Σ_j (1/P_ij).               (2)

In the homogeneous case with common g>1 and k, this is (g-1)min_i c_i-k. A rare useful opportunity for any one member can set the price ceiling for the entire circle. Without sufficient aggregate gain around the circle, changing quotas or the labels of original cardinal utility scales cannot deliver a strict gain to all.

Let only one party b bear a further installation expense F. For each i let j(i,b) be the number of steps in {1,...,m} needed to reach b. Formula (1) gives the exact upper limit, before strictness,

    F_max(b) = min_i P_i,j(i,b) [(1-1/G)c_i - Σ_j k_(i+j)/P_ij]. (3)

When all original margins are initially positive, a strictly acceptable compensation plan exists iff F<F_max(b). This ceiling can differ by builder even if its technology and outlay have the same cost. “Compensation” here can consist entirely of scarce future production in states where that party has a use for it. A cash transfer is unnecessary. The underlying risk increments, outside offers and real opportunities to change the flows set the ceiling.

## Same hardware expense, different willingness to construct it

Take three states with edges A→B, B→C, C→A and probabilities (1/12,1/3,1/3); the remaining probability is an unrelated productive state. A donor forgoes one unit of original value to deliver two units at the compatible recipient, so g_i=2. Without the A→B connection there is no return cycle and no arrangement of the remaining referrals gives everyone a strict gain over independence. Assume the first edge's sender A or its receiver B has the physical right and resources to install a missing adapter, at a foregone local output F=1/5. The values may represent counts of completed jobs with marginal values stable over this range, or the corresponding normalized original utility increments. Neither endpoint initially values somebody else's jobs.

With the new link and k_i=0, (3) gives gross builder ceilings of 7/12 for A, 7/48 for B and 7/24 for C. C's number is a valuation of additional referrals, not permission to alter the other two sites' machinery. Equal treatment gives each 1/12 before construction costs, too little to induce a sole builder at price 1/5. Referring every job on every route would instead give B a *loss* of 1/6. The parties can actively design a mutually acceptable alternative.

Let A fund the adapter and set activated expected donor expenditures to (1/12,1/8,5/24). Relative to independence, the originals get (1/3,1/24,1/24) before construction, hence (2/15,1/24,1/24) after A pays. Every founder would also cover a sufficiently small positive recognition and update fee from these margins. In contrast, even allowing all feasible referral lotteries and unequal terms, B cannot pay F and leave the others strictly above baseline, since 1/5>7/48. Reversals between these cases are strict and survive small perturbations. The proposed underwriter and the chooser's own existing objectives matter to whether the environment will actually be built.

One shared goal with weights (1,4,2) on A, B and C's original outcomes supports the allocation. A strictly refers on its new route. B and C are indifferent and use public probabilities 3/8 and 5/8 on theirs. This is a real change for all three: old B and C strictly preferred keeping even an assigned referral. A's decision to construct can also be made *after* conditional installation. Under its then-current weighted objective, the new edge increases the attainable weighted score by 7/12; building costs 1/5 in these weighted units. It strictly prefers to complete the adapter. A hypothetical B-builder under that same goal would incur weighted expense 4/5. Preparation consumes resources and there are states in which the new adapter is never needed. The original comparison already prices that probability.

An implementable offer before the state draw can name the active parties, builder and objective. Conditional activation needs valid assent from everyone, before anyone incurs the unrecoverable construction outlay. If refusal closes the agreement window, strictly positive founder margins give each signer a reason to accept, including A. The activated A then builds under its new goal, and when a job arrives all three follow their current routing incentives. If building must occur first, a recipient that can later hold out for a better offer can change A's incentive to spend; a binding prior subscription or genuine pre-installation repairs that timing when actually affordable. A cheaper binding ordinary router with the same routing and construction authority matches this example. If the ordinary routes are just unprotected promises in a single mutually exclusive opportunity, the original donor always prefers local service at execution. Assuming a paid local delegate or controlled inventory is feasible changes the comparison and should change the required k_i.

**Naturality and scope.** Familiar differences in available tools, deadlines or compatible job classes can make the same unit worth more at a recipient in some states, while another directed route returns a different favor. The factor of two is illustrative. The consequential facts are the cycle product, the capacities and the sponsor's attainable return. If physical output never changes and each site's value is just a constant multiple of the same homogeneous transferable output, the gain factors telescope around a cycle; real routing loss then makes their product less than one. Reciprocal gains in that environment require another source, such as state-dependent marginal use, insurance or a new compatible task. Merely writing high gains on every arrow would assume the answer. Parties that own the endpoints and expect the revenues in (3) have a reason to convert an absent arrow into real physical capacity.

**Exploration ledger.** Framing: future founders can intentionally create their own reason to accommodate. Structural: locate the bottleneck and price a builder's compensation in the other parties' actual future services. The three-site funded construction and the refusal by the other feasible builder are promising at this instance. “A project worth building for the coalition makes every capable site willing to sponsor it” is dead here. Its sibling identifies a willing endpoint and changes the division of referrals. Its structural successor searches over joint funding, timing of installation and multiple candidate partners, including the cost of discovering them and the authority needed to execute the chosen objective.
