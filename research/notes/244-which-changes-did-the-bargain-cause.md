# Which changes did the bargain cause?

A buyer may have good reason to trust a genuinely helpful controller and still have little evidence that *this particular bargain* changed its goal. A past and present observation can establish a historical change. Saying what would have happened without the offer requires a comparison with the other opportunities that agent would have taken.

## What one can learn from markets, including the applicants who never sign

Fix a target ordering, domain, date, feasible alternatives and a specified equilibrium selection for each of two offer regimes. For a given founder and external random draws let Y₁ be one if the target would be active with the eligible offer, and Y₀ the same indicator without it. Agents choose whether to sign and what to install in each regime. A fixed rule for potential physical outcomes is needed for an individual causal question. Different ways of coupling random installation failures across regimes can have the same observed marginals.

Suppose comparably drawn, independent markets can be assigned to the two regimes and their dated final goals measured. Measure all eligible sites; signing itself can depend on the offer. If other agents can be affected by an offer, define the intervention and measured unit at the appropriate market level. Let p₁ and p₀ be the resulting marginal probabilities. The fraction newly compatible *because of the offer*, η=Pr(Y₁=1,Y₀=0), has the sharp bounds

    max(0,p₁−p₀) ≤ η ≤ min(p₁,1−p₀).                               (244.1)

Indeed the four cell masses for (Y₁,Y₀)=(1,0),(1,1),(0,1),(0,0) are respectively η,p₁−η,p₀−p₁+η,1−p₀−η. Their nonnegativity gives the interval, and every point in it yields a valid distribution. Among genuine recipients in the assigned offer regime, the probability of causal conversion is η/p₁ under random assignment. A sample of voluntary signers alone warrants a different denominator. If the offer never turns a would-be compatible site into an incompatible one, η=p₁−p₀. Exclusive opportunities can violate that premise: a founder accepting a profitable ordinary output contract can give up a different partnership that would have required a genuine edit. Both choices can be voluntary and privately optimal.

For example p₁=.8 and p₀=.6 put the causal fraction among the genuinely compatible offered sites between .25 and .50. Perfect current-state measurements leave that interval. A trustworthy pre-state for the same site also answers whether it *actually* crossed the boundary during these dates. Even a witnessed crossing might have taken place under a different agreement in the absence of this offer. These are the classical potential-outcome bounds applied to actual terminal preferences. Measuring them in a population has its own participation and privacy costs. A randomized conservation-payment program illustrates the comparable practical question of how much extra outcome a payment purchased; it measured tree cover. [Tian and Pearl, §4](https://ftp.cs.ucla.edu/pub/stat_ser/r271-A.pdf) · [Jayachandran and coauthors](https://seemajayachandran.com/cashforcarbon.pdf)

## Giving people a reason to manufacture a true baseline transition

Consider a customer that owns the downstream benefit from an already operating line. It places s units of transferable stock in custody, payable on **two exact readings** giving an initial own-only goal N followed by the jointly named goal G. Without the grant a share b is already G and keeps serving. The others remain N. Peace leaves two units of base stock with each party. The terminal service choice **adds** own output a and buyer output Y, or own output a+d and buyer output zero. G values own stock plus θ times B's, with θY>d, while N values only own stock. A change from N to G costs k. Reverting G to N before the baseline reading costs r. Costs use the supplier's own resources. Once the program starts, B cannot recover the grant by reneging. A contest interrupts the line and leaves 1.5 expected units per side after recovery, below even the peaceful base stocks. For instance take a=4, d=1 and Y=4. The downstream service belongs to its controller and runs by default, so B cannot shut down the existing G work by refusing a grant.

An original N accepts and pays for a genuine change when s>d+k. A pre-existing G can leave its goal untouched or first revert, appear truthfully as N, and then apply to change back. Once it has reverted, its then-current N will indeed pay k to restore G under that same inequality. The original G values a grant taken from B at (1−θ)s, and both costly edits reduce its own stocks. Hence it strictly chooses the round trip when (1−θ)s>r+k. All measured goal changes between the two readings are real. The customer has bought extra lasting compatibility only from the originally incompatible sites.

A grant that strictly attracts N while strictly deterring the round trip exists exactly when d+k<(r+k)/(1−θ) for 0<θ<1, subject to available posted prices. For θ=.5, d=1, k=.4, r=.2 the lower threshold 1.4 exceeds the upper threshold 1.2. Every grant large enough to attract N also attracts G into the two extra edits under the announced-baseline protocol. This obstruction covers the amount of that grant, with the stated timing and resources. An earlier physical observation repairs it.

Let B offer s on a half-unit grid starting at zero, and let posting and reading cost .10 per eligible site if the program operates. The least strict inducing grant is 1.5. At b=.30 it yields B (1−b)4−1.5−.10=1.20 above no program. Smaller positive prices attract no new service; larger prices give the same new service at a higher bill. Every final G is certified and every funded site really switched N→G. Only .70 of all sites gained the target relative to no program. The others had temporarily given it up. At b=.80 even the lowest inducing grant loses money, so B declines this version.

Suppose a separate custodian can provide an accurate, stable, *pre-announcement* reading at an additional .12 per eligible site. Applicants must release their archived status to claim the new grant. A pre-existing G cannot qualify by changing today. Each genuine N authorizes access and changes; G has no reason to revert. This leaves B (1−b)(4−1.5)−.10−.12. At b=.80 that is **.28**, enough to operate and induce genuine changes at the previously unserved fifth of sites. At b=.30 it gives **1.53**. If an existing guarantee of the old grant requires consent from prime beneficiaries, a prealigned G's former extra utility was .5×1.5−.6=.15. Paying each archived G .31 gives it .155 in the same current-goal units. At b=.30 that costs B .093 and leaves it ahead of the former 1.20. N still receives the same grant and terms. An archive that holders could rewrite, anticipate when choosing their old goal or sell only selectively without affecting eligibility would need a different assessment.

**What the customer actually values.** A budget paid for extra productive service gives B a reason to distinguish already helpful sites from additional ones. If it only needs one observable shipment, an ordinary reward larger than d can secure delivery from an unchanged N without paying k. With a cheap output meter that route can win. If the customer needs durable service on many subsequently unmeasured jobs, the price of an ordinary binding controller, the true persistence of the change and options for later revision matter again. Trustworthy independent dated observations are a strong technical premise; deliberate baseline shifts are cheaply possible only if the old G can actually reverse and the later N would privately choose to restore. The counterfactual and the physical measurements serve different questions.
