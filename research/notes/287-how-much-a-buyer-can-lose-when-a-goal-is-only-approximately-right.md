# 287 — How much can a buyer lose when a goal is only approximately right?

A buyer can know that a supplier's preferences differ from the agreed ones and still have a reason to turn over the project. What matters for that decision is how much of the buyer's valued output a possible disagreement puts at risk. A single probability of a perfectly correct installation leaves out that magnitude.

## A sharp loss over every choice of production lotteries

Suppose the agreement covers objective lotteries over a known finite list of grounded physical outcomes. Write f for the target's scores and g for the installed scores on that list. If the successor prefers lottery Q at least as much as P, the target loses f(P)−f(Q) when the successor selects Q. The largest loss over all possible menus, allowing either action at a tie, is

    R(f|g) = max { f(P)−f(Q) : g(P)≤g(Q) }
           = min over a≥0 of [ max_i(f_i−a g_i) − min_i(f_i−a g_i) ].    (287.1)

To prove it, represent both lotteries by their nonnegative probability vectors. For any allowed pair and a≥0, its target loss is bounded by the difference between the largest and smallest coordinates of f−ag. For equality, dualize the finite linear program on the first line. The two probability-sum constraints give unrestricted intercepts u and v; the one comparison gives multiplier a≥0. The constraints on individual probabilities reduce to u≥f_i−ag_i and v≥−f_i+ag_i. Minimizing u+v gives the second line. The primal is feasible and compact. At an optimal extreme point its two probability constraints and at most one active comparison allow at most three positive probability entries in total. Thus two small lotteries attain the full price of a disagreement. If the target is constant, every choice has zero target loss even if the successor has definite preferences. For a nonconstant target, zero loss requires a positive affine relation and hence the exact preference agreement of [286](286-checking-an-agreed-ordering-without-a-shared-utility-scale.md).

The corresponding question about regret for policies in a fixed decision process is established in the reward-comparison literature. [Skalse and colleagues, Definitions 5–6 and discussion of reachable lotteries](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.15257)

Sometimes the only physically attainable lotteries are mixtures of a finite set of production recipes. Apply (287.1) to the two columns of expected scores *on those recipes*. A randomized menu gives any point of their convex hull. Substituting those columns in the same lottery proof gives the sharp loss, with at most three recipes in a witness. If only a fixed discrete menu is available and the parties cannot randomize, checking its actual pairs may give a smaller loss. Possessing the same event alphabet does not establish the same attainable menu. For the three grades below, if the line offers only the certain medium and a .70 high lottery, the worst attainable target loss is .20. It rises to .30 if the jobs can include the .80 high lottery that makes the two options tie under g.

The bound is invariant under independent changes of the installed score's positive scale and origin. Multiplying the *target* scale multiplies the loss in target units, as it should. For example, let f=(0,.5,1) and g=(0,.8,1). Both prefer high to medium to low. At a=1 the bound is .30. A lottery with .80 chance of the high outcome and .20 of the low one ties the certain medium under g but produces .30 more expected target value. A slightly smaller chance of high gives a strict disagreement with almost the same loss. This is a difference in preference over lotteries even when a trial between any two unrandomized products would pass.

If an inspector can warrant only that each true g_i is within η of its measured value, substituting the measurements in the right side and adding 2aη *inside* the minimization gives a safe upper bound. This follows by allowing either end of the residual range to move by aη. It is an economic guarantee even when the actual table is unknown. It applies to the specified outcomes. Approximate agreement just on the short algebraic basis of 286 can be misleading for an unlimited event domain. A constant installed score and a target scoring k holding periods at (1−ε)^k differ by only ε between their two necessary basis histories, empty and one hold. Their largest possible target loss through T holds is 1−(1−ε)^T, approaching one. Physical bounds on job length or operating dynamics can price that additional exposure. ## A supplier and customer who would both sign

A shared production line offers three terminal grades. At grade with score f_i the original Blue receives f_i units, and the original Red receives 4−2f_i units. Its founder therefore ranks low above high. The parties propose installing the grade-ordering f in Red's final production controller. The on-line choices are to keep the low grade, produce the medium grade with certainty, or run a batch with .70 chance of high and .30 of low. Physical conversion makes the realized lots irreversible. Before routing, either party can start a disruptive contest with a strength-dependent allocation of salvaged material. The available salvage pays at most .20 blue units and 1.50 red units, below the credible continuations for the party deciding. Before entrustment Blue can select separate equipment, giving Blue .50 and Red 2.50. Entrustment commits those separate inputs. At the final converter the operating choices are the three recipes or disruptive salvage. Extend the partially aligned grade criterion monotonically below the medium grade, assigning any such salvage less value than medium.

A registered operation costs Red .20 and Blue .01 for the witness. After a real attempt the installed goal is exactly f with probability .60, the g above with probability .25, and Red's old ranking with probability .15. Either founder can refuse at signing. Blue can restrict use of this owned line to physically witnessed procedures and freely declines an unwitnessed claim, on which an unchanged supplier would keep the low grade. It learns that the procedure happened and lacks a readout of the realized goal. If an omitted procedure could produce the same witness, the supplier would prefer to skip and keep the lot.

The target's best feasible run returns .70 in expectation. Even using only the all-menu bound, Blue can count on at least .60×.70+.25×(.70−.30)=.52, enough to cover the .50 outside and .01 charge. Using the particular menu, the approximately correct controller prefers the certain medium to the risky run. Blue actually receives .60×.70+.25×.50−.01=.535. Red receives 4−2×.545−.20=2.71. Both original owners strictly prefer the arrangement. Some successful runs still yield the low grade through physical chance. The likelihoods of a real procedure, any genuine change of ordering and the exact negotiated ordering per signed batch are **1, .85 and .60**, respectively. The .25 of agents with a different ordering cannot be reported as exact adoption. They can willingly be given work under a bargain that acknowledges the risk.

If only the total .85 undergoing some change is known, let q be its exact-goal mass *per signed batch*. With this known menu the lower endpoint of the admissible q interval suffices for strict customer consent when **q>.425**: the gross blue return is .85×.50+q×.20. If the only assurance is the all-menu .30 loss bound and a .70 target opportunity, **q>17/30** suffices. These are different information sets. Knowing the actual available lotteries can justify operating at a lower confidence in exact installation. Red's outside and .20 bill are covered even at q=.85 in these calculations.

An ordinary router with identical rights and access can select the .70 run without any change of preferences. Denote its physical expense in founder units by c, paid by Blue. With transferable working stock it can improve on the uncertain agreement for both exactly when 2.60+.70−c>2.71+.535, or **c<.055**. At c=.03 an advance .12 payment from Blue to Red gives 2.72 and .55. At c=.10 it cannot make both founders prefer switching at these prices. This compares the same output, forecast and contest opportunities, including the incumbents' rights to refuse the amendment.

**Where these conditions could arise.** Batches with several useful grades, uncertain quality and optional operating recipes provide a physical reason to care about ranked lotteries. A cheap commissioning witness can be attractive when it authenticates work locally but cannot inspect the eventual preference and actuator remotely. The three-point error law and its reliability, enforceable access to the line, the similarly graded payoffs and relative prices of an ordinary router are assumptions. An error that makes an entirely different resource attractive to the supplier or an operator who can introduce new grades widens the reachable loss and can justify rejection or a narrower first order. The example gives a constructive sufficient level of assurance together with a price at which the original owners would deliberately choose a different institution.
