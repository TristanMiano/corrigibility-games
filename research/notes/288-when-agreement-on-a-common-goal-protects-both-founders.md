# 288 — When does agreement on a common goal protect both founders?

A common goal can put the same value on two allocations that matter very differently to the original owners. An accurate certificate then leaves a distributional question for the founders. When useful production itself varies across locations, they can sometimes deliberately make the joint goal select a mutually attractive allocation and continue to work with substantial uncertainty about both the goal and the location.

## Whose loss does an accurate goal measure?

Let f be the negotiated objective, b one founder's incumbent valuation, both expected scores on the same finite outcome set. Suppose a target-optimal choice P and a possible successor choice Q differ by at most τ≥0 in expected target value. The sharp upper bound on the founder's loss, uniformly across all such pairs of lotteries, is

    K_b(τ) = max { b(P)−b(Q) : 0≤f(P)−f(Q)≤τ }
           = min over a real of [range(b−a f)+τ max(0,a)].           (288.1)

Here range is maximum minus minimum over the outcomes. Dualizing the two inequalities on the difference introduces nonnegative multipliers λ,μ. For a=λ−μ, the cheapest price for the upper tolerance is λτ=max(0,a)τ. The two lottery-sum constraints give the residual range as in [287](287-how-much-a-buyer-can-lose-when-a-goal-is-only-approximately-right.md). The finite feasible set of probability pairs is compact. The formula is attained over pairs: offer just the two lotteries and let the allowed choice favor Q. A particular certified program that must maximize its own specified goal on a fixed larger menu can have a smaller loss. This bound needs only the stated target-value tolerance. It works as a sufficient input to a founder's consent test.

At τ=0 every founder who assigns different values to two target-indifferent lotteries faces residual exposure. It disappears for all such comparisons exactly when b=af+c for some *real* a,c. If f is constant, this requires b to be constant too. If b is constant and f varies, take a=0. When both founders' output interests differ from the common goal in this sense, even perfect certainty about f leaves a reason to negotiate the choice set or distribution of control. A guarantee of small loss in joint output is much stronger for a founder that already values almost the same allocations. Checking (288.1) for both incumbent valuations makes that distinction explicit.

## The buyer and supplier can buy a reason to choose a productive allocation

One batch of common raw stock yields either (8 red, 2 blue) or (2 red, 8 blue). Each founder values only its own color. A merger is unnecessary: Red's controller operates the recipe and Blue can retain its independent projects. At commissioning they propose an actual change in Red's terminal objective to total usable stock. With two ten-unit recipes this successor is indifferent. Even an exact proof of its goal permits selection of the red-heavy recipe every time. Substituting the scores (10,10) for f and (2,8) for Blue in (288.1) gives a six-unit exposure at zero target error. If the agreed target instead counted Blue's own output alone, that exposure at tolerance τ would fall to min(τ,6); the original Red would still have to be paid for consenting to such terms. In a working assessment that splits the equally ranked recipes fairly, both receive five. Giving them equal shares of the *physical authority* through an ordinary coin also induces that allocation with unchanged goals, whenever the equitable draw controls which owner can act.

A .30-unit equipment purchase by Red can put an extra recoverable unit into the heavy color of exactly one recipe, chosen with equal probability. In practice, a transport or a mounting decision can make a little extra productive material available along one stream after the owners have committed. A local indication identifies the favored recipe with symmetric accuracy r>.5. Both owners know its quality. Only the current operator sees the indication before the irreversible conversion. A common-goal successor now strictly follows the indication: it gains 2r−1 expected units of total output compared with the contrary recipe. If the two choices' computed expected totals can each err by at most η, the ranking remains strict whenever **2η<2r−1**. An unchanged Red still always uses the red-heavy recipe. The physical asymmetry makes the distinction between those actual preferences consequential.

A witnessed real attempt costs Red .10 and the witness costs Blue .05. Its success probability is q and success is independent of the batch and forecast. The founders decide and settle any fixed enrollment transfer before they can learn either outcome. Blue can reserve the job for an authenticated attempt. An otherwise costless bare declaration supplies no such right. At the start they can return to their own lines, yielding 4.8 red and 4.4 blue. Once committed, either still may disrupt the plant before conversion, with a winner determined by strength recovering at most 1.5 of either color. Every permissible peaceful recipe leaves at least two units of each. A party cannot seize the enrollment transfer by later contest. Interpreting a change in budget or control as credible therefore requires these timing and property rights.

When the goal changes, symmetry gives each original founder 5+r/2 on average. With Red's original goal it gets 8.5, while Blue gets two. Including both expenses and the .30 equipment purchase, but before transfers, expected original stocks are

    Red  = 8.5 − q(3.5−r/2) − .40,
    Blue = 2 + q(3+r/2) − .05.                                    (288.2)

Consider the best ordinary *uninformed* authority lottery using the same equipment, with equal appointments as a reference. Each gross expectation is 5.25, because the authority holder always picks its own heavy color and the favorable bay is independent of appointment. After the .30 equipment bill the returns are 4.95 and 5.25. Biased appointments move resources between the partners and have the same total. Any ordinary authority policy fixed before the local indication has the same half chance of exploiting the extra unit. With free flat transfers the uncertain change can improve on every such division in total precisely when

    q(r−.5) > .15.                                                  (288.3)

This is the additional probability of recovering the extra unit, weighed against the two fees. Winning both vetoes still requires an available transfer satisfying each comparison against the actual default and, when the safe lines are available, those payoffs too.

At q=r=.80 the uncertain change yields (5.62,4.67) before transfer. Red offers .60 to Blue for signing conditional on the witnessed attempt. The owners get **5.02 and 5.27**. Each exceeds its equipped ordinary fair-appointment option and the five each obtained from a free fair appointment without the equipment. The original Red pays for an .80 chance of genuinely caring about both stocks, for the equipment that makes that goal informative, and for the weaker beneficiary's consent. Even conditional on the correct goal, probability .20 remains that the forecast sends work to the less productive recipe. Only .64 of batches have both an actual change and a correct operating forecast. Knowing the numeric ordering exactly after installation would leave the latter uncertainty.

If instead a capable ordinary router can follow the *same private indication*, both founders can obtain 5.40 before the equipment and its own installation expenses, while keeping their original preferences. The total is 10.50−c when Red pays .30 and the router costs Blue c. It can beat the negotiated change with appropriate division at **c<.21**. At c=.10 it gives Red 5.10 and Blue 5.30 even without an additional transfer. At c=.30 its total falls below the voluntarily changed agreement. If an operator can cheaply commit to follow or reveal the local indication, that is another way to implement this ordinary control. If the equipment's location could be chosen by the initially favored owner after registration, the calculation for a fair location would need an incentive to preserve the draw.

**Naturality and reach.** Allocations with the same total but different beneficiaries occur whenever one scarce machine can favor either of two owners. Local grade differences and forecasts can reward a common objective at a costed, real decision. Random placement after signing, portable enrollment stock, a protected local indication and the relative prices of modifying a running evaluator and binding a separate router are the stronger premises here. Smaller benefits, an inaccurate forecast or a cheap control can move the same founders to ordinary compromise. Whether verification is worth buying depends jointly on what the actual goal ranks, what physical options those rankings select, and what those options do for *each* original signer.
