# What learning your own result tells you about the partner

Two sincere plants may use the same unreliable service, or compete for the same scarce technician. An observation of one's own failed update then changes what one can infer about the *other* plant. The sign depends on how their physical chances of success are linked.

## Correlated installations, separate observations

Let q_A,q_B be the marginal success probabilities when both attempt and J the probability of both successes. Assume both chose before privately learning which commissioning opportunities were available. With c=J−q_Aq_B, A's prior probability that B succeeded is q_B+c/q_A upon learning A succeeded, and q_B−c/(1−q_A) upon learning A failed. These identities follow by splitting the joint table. A subsequent positive B-reading multiplies B's odds by t_B/e_B when the reading law depends only on B's actual state. A truthful report about A's own state thus has different information content under a common environmental constraint and under competition for a fixed resource.

If only both positive readings are public and their measurement errors are independent conditional on the two installations, an outsider's posterior for *both* successes is

    J t_A t_B /
    [e_A e_B + q_A(t_A−e_A)e_B + q_B(t_B−e_B)e_A
               + J(t_A−e_A)(t_B−e_B)].                          (214.1)

This strictly increases in J for interior discriminating tests and positive prior marginals. At fixed marginals the sharp extremes are obtained by substituting max(0,q_A+q_B−1) or min(q_A,q_B) for J. A shared measurement fault would change this formula even for fixed installations.

Take q_A=q_B=.6, t=.9 and e=.2. In a good or bad service season with equal chances, both sites' success rates are .9 or .3, conditionally independent given the season. This gives J=.45. A that knows it succeeded gives a positive B-reading probability **27/29** of a genuine B. An A that knows it failed gives the very same B-reading **27/37**. The outside observer given both positives gives joint success probability about .851. Instead allocate enough windows for both with probability .2 and for just one subscriber, chosen fairly, with probability .8. Then J=.2 with the same individual capabilities. A that succeeded now credits B's positive only **9/13**. A that failed knows B had a window. The two-positive joint probability is about .529. All of these posteriors can arise on paths where every signer sincerely tried.

## Do the founders actually choose the attempts?

Use the two-sided production and public opening rule of [213](213-both-partners-have-to-change.md). First suppose the reserved service rights expire if unused; declining one's own use does not increase the partner's chance of success. The difference between attempting and falsely signing, under i's *original* criterion and before expense, is

    J m_i − q_i n_i,                                              (214.2)

where m_i=t_j b_i(t_i−e_i)−t_i d_i(t_j−e_j) and n_i=t_i d_i e_j. Condition on both potential successes, when a genuinely configured member can get in more often and must contribute if open. Then condition on i's potential success alone, when false admission of j can cost i a contribution. On a potential failure of i, attempting and faking yield the same active decisions. This proves (214.2). If j attempts with probability x_j independently of the hidden service draw, replace J by x_j J in (214.2), with the same q_i n_i subtraction. The option of refusing, and the fee for subscribing, still require participation checks.

With b_i=5,d_i=1 for both, m_i=2.52 and n_i=.18. Sincere attempt margins before cost are 1.026 in the shared season and .396 in the scarce-window example. At a private expense of .35 both founders strictly try in the mutual-attempt assessment of both environments. Even false signing can earn q_j e_i t_j b_i=.54 from a helpful partner, so a common .05 gate subscription is affordable. At an expense of .6, the shared-season arrangement still supports reciprocal change; the scarce-window one has an attempt margin below cost even when the other certainly tries. In this case every smaller x_j further reduces the return, so that specified gate supports no positive attempt rate. A higher-powered contest with better recovery or a binding ordinary router is an additional alternative.

If an unused slot automatically helps the other plant even while the declaration of readiness is private, a false signer gets additional expected work. Let the partner's success when i conserves its resource rise by Δ≥0. The all-attempt margin in (214.2) falls by Δ e_i t_j b_i. At Δ=.4 in the scarce example, the margin falls from .396 to .036. With an observable slot release, the other's inference and admission rules must be resolved. The deployment and visibility of the saved resource matter separately.

## Buying coordinated availability

Plants can have a reason to schedule *the same* service windows. Suppose matching the dates increases joint availability from J=.20 to .45, holding each plant's marginal at .6, with local work displaced worth .2 to each. These joint tables are implementable by a public scheduler reserving both, single or no windows at the specified rates; actual availability is revealed after the configuration choice. Each site's payoff on the mutual-attempt path before setup equals q_j e_i t_j b_i + Jm_i −q_i n_i. If attempts already occur, each therefore gains ( .45−.20 )×2.52=.63 before the .2 charge. Both can strictly fund the calendar. When the original .6 expense excluded any scarce-window attempts, the synchronized upper equilibrium gives each .54+1.026−.6−.2=.766 before any common gate fee. Coordination and credible timing are needed to implement the advantageous selection.

**Naturality and reach.** Independent project schedules, joint maintenance and shared specialists give concrete reasons for positive or negative dependence. Evidence channels are still assumed grounded in the relevant evaluator. A purchaser can pay for synchronized availability because useful service depends on *both* configurations. This calculation fixes forgone local work and who controls scheduling; correlation is not assumed freely adjustable by each user. Conventional binding services should be assigned the same calendar when feasible. The sibling compares a transferable technician with an expiring appointment. The broader question asks who has an incentive to reveal private information about a shared capability before the two parties make irreversible changes.
