# How much reserve lets a buyer keep taking risky bargains?

A buyer can voluntarily keep accepting agreements that it knows will sometimes fail. What a run of failures could do to its remaining plant matters separately from how sure it is that *this* supplier changed. This note gives a resource and preference threshold for risking the last spare, and prices an extra spare that lets the parties make more genuine changes.

## Each supplier gets one batch, the buyer has a continuing plant

Keep the one-batch supplier's original returns from 206: two on peaceful refusal, four if it delivers, six if it keeps. New suppliers' private installation costs are independently uniform on [0,1.4], and an honest attempt succeeds with probability .8. The .9/.1 commissioning reader costs the buyer .1 per offered job. A delivered batch produces two blue units with probability .9 and zero otherwise; retention produces zero. A changed supplier's terminal goal weights two blue units of value per unit of blue output alongside its own red. It prefers attempting delivery, since 4+2(.9)(2)>6. The buyer can also safely return its batch, producing one dated blue unit instead. Recovering contested stock costs the processing window and yields at most half a unit of each output in expectation, including for the winner within this short window. The safe return is worth more than contest to either controller before an irreversible processing choice. After release the supplier acts before the buyer can take the batch back. The red supplier cares about its own dated job. The blue buyer cares about a succession of jobs.

The buyer has r interchangeable spare units. A successful release replenishes one net spare and earns +1 dated blue output over safe return. An unsuccessful release consumes one net spare and earns −1. For example, a good batch can include two usable replacement cartridges as a byproduct of the blue output, having consumed one cartridge. The safe route consumes none. The reader draws from a separate operating account. Running out of spares destroys a further valuable production opportunity. At the next date the buyer pays a one-time original-utility loss L≥0 and can do no further commissioning. It can keep the spares and retire early without that loss. The buyer discounts dated output at D=.9. The extra loss is a specified value of keeping a separate capability, with no second terminal payment for the counted routine blue output.

Suppose the buyer purchases the test while operating and releases only positives. Each fresh founder then signs and has the same cutoff 1.12 as in 206. The pre-change decision depends on red output. The proportion genuinely changed is .64 per offered job. The positive frequency is k=153/250. Conditional on a positive, actual goal confidence is 16/17 and production succeeds with probability u=72/85. A negative succeeds with probability n=72/485 if released. The receiver must still prefer buying this instrument at each reserve level. Returning today's batch and keeping the option on tomorrow is available.

## The buyer's choice at every reserve level

Let V_r be the buyer's discounted value above the outside output stream, before seeing today's reading. Under the proposed policy the one-step equation is

    V_r=−c+k(2u−1)+D[k u V_(r+1)+k(1−u)V_(r−1)+(1−k)V_r],    V_0=−L.

Put V∞=[k(2u−1)−c]/(1−D)=3.248 and d=Dk/[1−D(1−k)]. The smaller root α of α=d[uα²+1−u] is .14438622. The bounded solution is V_r=V∞−(V∞+L)α^r, as substitution shows. Bounded discounted returns give uniqueness by contraction. Compared with declining and revisiting the same state tomorrow, a release with success probability z has incremental value

    H_z(r)=2z−1+D(V∞+L)α^(r−1)(1−α)[zα−(1−z)].

Here α<(1−u)/u since d<1 and u>1/2. The risk deduction is therefore largest at the last spare for both u and n. Paid positive-only inspection beats always declining exactly when k H_u(r)>c, and beats blind release exactly when −(1−k)H_n(r)>c. As n<1/2 the second bound holds at every r when c<(1−k)(1−2n)=.2728. The first bound is tightest at r=1. By the value equation it is equivalent there to V_1>0. Thus for the stated technology, whenever

    L < V∞(1/α−1) ≈ 19.2472,                                    (253.1)

buying the test, risking the last spare on a positive, and renewing the offer tomorrow are strict buyer best responses at every positive reserve. The inequalities also give strict signal responses. Together with the original suppliers' incentives they support this positive assessment. Larger losses than (253.1) rule out *this particular policy* at the last spare. A buyer may select other contracts, and coordination on inactivity remains possible.

For a large plant loss, say L=110, the buyer will protect its last spare even if a perfectly informative observer identifies a genuine controller there. Any allowed release succeeds with probability at most v=.9. The largest possible future gain above safe output before another ruin is 1/(1−D)=10. Risking the last spare gives at most 2v−1+Dv/(1−D)−D(1−v)L<0 before any bill. Returning or retiring safely gives at least zero. With r≥2, check positive-only inspection against the shifted value V_r=V∞[1−α^(r−1)], since retirement at one has value zero. This is precisely the previous calculation with L=0 shifted up one unit; the same strict purchase and release inequalities apply everywhere in this range. Suppliers with an active offer still genuinely change in 64% of jobs. An unused spare now protects the other production opportunity.

## What confidence about a whole trading relationship costs

The probability of ever depleting r available risk units is [(1−u)/u]^r=(13/72)^r on the continuously operating policy with independent new suppliers. To verify it, absorb the walk at zero or at M, solve the two-boundary recursion with roots 1 and (1−u)/u, then take M to infinity. Waiting for a positive changes the calendar, not those hitting probabilities. With low L and three spares, physical ruin occurs with probability .00589 despite independent failures on an indefinitely continuing line. With high L the buyer never risks physical ruin. Its production program can still end when one spare remains. Starting with two or three spares, the probabilities of such an early retirement are respectively .18056 and .03260. Per-batch confidence is identical in these comparisons.

At high L a verifiable spare purchased before the next job increases the buyer's discounted operating value by V∞α^(r−1)(1−α). From two spares it will pay up to **.40125** for the third. A vendor with a .25 delivered price can thus induce actual purchase; under a common sequence of opportunities the extra buffer strictly extends trade on a positive-probability set. Each additional active offer has a .64 chance of creating a newly agreed goal. The discounted expected number of these additional installations is .64α(1−α)/(1−D), about .79. No change in the chemistry or goal confidence is needed for those extra agreements.

**Reach.** A finite buffer and a rare loss of an irreplaceable facility give independent reasons for the state and the preferences. The exact probabilities require replenishable units, independent fresh suppliers, consistent inspection and little scope for borrowing after a bad run. A router with the same stochastic governance, price and physical production law gives the same buyer reserve and original welfare. A buyer able to hire such a router more cheaply can displace edits, and backup credit or insurance with enforceable rights can displace retirement.
