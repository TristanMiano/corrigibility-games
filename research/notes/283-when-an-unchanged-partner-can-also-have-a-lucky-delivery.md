# 283 — What can repeated service prove when a retained partner can also get lucky?

Suppose unchanged and genuinely accommodating suppliers can both produce every observed outcome. No finite delivery record settles their goal with certainty. Nevertheless a buyer can start a profitable relationship, learn to high confidence, and eventually work with a genuine partner indefinitely. That conclusion depends on which histories it accepts, the supplier's own discount rate, and whether a fresh partner can actually be recruited.

## A fallible source with a persistent identity

Each identifiable recruit has an opportunity to make a witnessed, voluntary attempt at the agreed change, with success chance q=.75 and red cost k between .8 and 1.4. Its current goal then persists during the relationship. The customer owns successive input lots. Each fresh recruitment costs it f, including the first. Before each lot it can replace, return to safe work or release. The old supplier earns X=4 over return by keeping the batch; it can instead give up d=2 and earn a=2 by using the partner's production method. That method yields a positive blue outcome with chance v=.75. Even a retained lot can produce some useful blue byproduct, with chance e=.25. Success gives Blue R=2 over safe production and failure loses H=1. Responding with the partner's method gives expected blue output 1.5 units above retention. The negotiated successor values red plus twice blue, so giving up two red units is strictly worthwhile to it on a single job. Safe stocks and physical contest before irreversible processing are as in [253](253-how-much-reserve-lets-a-buyer-keep-taking-risky-bargains.md). The model keeps honest production failures and accidental good deliveries separate from whether the founder really changed.

On a candidate assessment G uses the method, N retains and conditional draws are fresh. If the record contains k′ more successes than failures, the buyer's odds on the agreed goal are [q/(1−q)]ρ^k′ with ρ=v/e=3. One success from intake gives 90% confidence, two successive successes give 27/28. All finite sequences have positive probability under either controller. A retained agent considering a costly demonstration knows its true goal and the same public rule for future assignments. Its incentive below determines whether those likelihoods are valid.

## Which jobs the buyer will risk

Let D be Blue's discount. A cutoff m≥1 means retaining the current partner until its net record first reaches −m, and then replacing it. For a controller with actual success rate p define

    r_p(D) = 2D(1−p) / [1+√(1−4D²p(1−p))].

At distance n above the dismissal boundary the discounted chance factor for its hitting time τ is E[D^τ; τ finite]=r_p(D)^n. Substituting this expression into the one-step recurrence gives r=D[p r²+1−p]. The boundary value is one and bounded discounted solutions on the half-line are unique by contraction. If V_p=[pR−(1−p)H]/(1−D), the expected discounted production before hitting that boundary is V_p(1−r_p^n). Each job is chosen before its fresh outcome is known. Summing this production and restarting on dismissal gives the time-zero value

    W_m = [−f + q V_v(1−r_v^m)+(1−q)V_e(1−r_e^m)]
          /[1−q r_v^m−(1−q)r_e^m].                              (283.1)

The buyer can compare these values globally. Its Bellman problem on the current posterior has a type-independent alternative to replace or retire. Both posterior updates are increasing in the incoming posterior, a success produces the larger update, and the chance of that success is itself increasing. The expectation of any increasing continuation value therefore increases with the posterior. Current production does too. Value iteration preserves this ordering. The optimal release region is consequently an upper interval. On the likelihood lattice all positive-start stationary choices are exactly these cutoffs, including never replacing at m=∞. At a live job the buyer may still contest; in this instance disruption destroys the productive opportunity and yields less than the safe stock to either side.

For an efficient check fix a candidate W_m between V_e and V_v. Write F_j=−f+q(V_v−W_m)(1−r_v^j)+(1−q)(V_e−W_m)(1−r_e^j). Its sign gives the sign of W_j−W_m for j≥1. The increments F_(j+1)−F_j are a positive constant times r_v^j minus another positive constant times r_e^j. Since r_v<r_e they change sign at most once, from positive to negative. Checking the two adjacent increments at m verifies the global maximum, including never replacing. Choosing no engagement is worth zero.

At D=.9 and f=.50 the optimum is m=1 and W≈10.55557. At f=2 the buyer wants m=2 and W≈8.38948. The adjacent increments are about (+.5000,−.16868) and (+.23284,−.14813) respectively. The underlying roots are r_v≈.276668 and r_e≈.830004. Even a shop with favorable posterior odds initially deserves a finite chance to disprove itself. At the dearer recruitment price the buyer will pay for an additional failed history. The last job it declines can still have positive expected current output, because a freshly recruited partner is more valuable.

## Would a retained supplier deliberately serve once?

Let δ be the supplier's discount and let n≥1 be its distance to dismissal. Its own value from continuing to retain is J_n=X(1−r_e(δ)^n)/(1−δ). Serving once increases today's success probability from e to v and costs d. Its advantage over retention is

    −d + δ(v−e)(J_(n+1)−J_(n−1)).

The difference in future access equals X(1−r_e(δ)²)r_e(δ)^(n−1)/(1−δ), largest at n=1. Thus strict retention at *every* active history holds exactly when d>δ(v−e)X(1−r_e(δ)²)/(1−δ). This checks every one-step deviation from a bounded discounted policy, so it checks the best dynamic response. The right side strictly increases with δ: at n=1 it is δ(v−e) times the discounted sum of strictly positive rents starting two states above dismissal. Substituting the numerical primitives in the equality, using r=δ(.25r²+.75), gives

    δ < (√73−3)/10 ≈ .55440                                      (283.2)

for strict separation. Above that value, at the last admitted history a retained supplier has a profitable deliberate demonstration. Using the old likelihoods to quote high confidence would then be unjustified. It takes a new assessment to decide what happens commercially. A genuine supplier strictly prefers serving: it gains immediately, retains access to its positive red income, and conditional on its true G, its future blue yield is at least the average yield on replacement business. Each recruit prefers the witnessed attempt at procurement because even its first job gives at least two red units before its k≤1.4 and later access has nonnegative red value. These are choices by the original and successor selves at their respective dates.

At δ=.20, both calculated buyer cutoffs are consequently supported. A retained supplier can have two straight lucky deliveries without misrepresentation. At the m=2 site, the probability of reaching record +2 before dismissal is .10 under N and .90 under G. These follow from the two-boundary random walk odds in [221](221-what-a-customer-does-with-the-raw-readings.md). The buyer arriving there rightly assigns 27/28 confidence. It can continue doing business while still allowing that the supplier has an unchanged goal.

## Which partners survive the repeated test?

Starting each new relationship at distance m, an actual G escapes dismissal forever with probability 1−ρ^(−m). This is the limit of the finite upper-boundary hitting formula as that upper boundary goes to infinity. An actual N is dismissed eventually with probability one by negative drift. Each independent recruit therefore has probability q(1−ρ^(−m)) of starting a lasting genuine relationship. Almost surely there is eventually one. On it the law of large numbers drives the posterior to one, conditional on continued operation. Every *finite* posterior remains strictly below one. The expected total genuine installations through that eventual relationship is 1/(1−ρ^(−m)), by renewal. It is 1.5 at m=1 and 1.125 at m=2. A buyer demanding more frequent replacement can induce more new founders to change even when both eventual operations are equally genuine. This is a single buyer with indefinitely available, independent recruits and a stable goal. A mortal site or a supplier with a profitable reversal changes the persistence question.

For each initial supplier at δ=.20, even k=0 would give at most [q a+(1−q)X]/(1−δ)=3.125 red units over outside before the attempt bill. On the specified k≥.8 range its best such net value is below 2.325 or equal at that upper bound. A certain *ordinary* controller capable of choosing the same shipping method on each future lot gives Red 2.50. Blue then gets V_v−f−c/(1−D), including the same recruitment charge and a per-job rental c. At c=.10 this is 11 and 9.5 for the two markets above, respectively. In either market Blue and the initial Red strictly prefer this persistent ordinary arrangement. A later supplier entitled to an assured job could have its own veto. If no affordable router has continuing authority over the private method, the negotiated changes remain actual profitable choices despite the accidental successes and failures.

**Naturality and remaining scope.** Byproducts, noisy final measurements, regular trading partners and costly recruitment each give physical reasons for positive results from more than one goal. Identifiable continuing suppliers can rationally serve differently as future orders become valuable. Which source produced a favorable result therefore depends on patience as well as measurement error. Public attempt receipts, constant symmetric likelihoods, a bound on private setup expense and indefinite survival of the particular controller are special assumptions. The nearest repair is a priced right to recheck a continuing partner. Repeated competitive entry by *providers* of verifiers and ordinary controllers remains a wider question than the exogenous supply of new production partners here.
