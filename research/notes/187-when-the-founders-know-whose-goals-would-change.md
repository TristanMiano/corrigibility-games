# When the founders already know whose goals would change

**E8, note 187.** The minimum-change role lottery in 183 can ask a site to consent before it knows it will never receive another booking. Once the proposed participants know their identities, the group must actually pay the founders who will change. A renewable schedule can do that, but it needs a longer horizon or more genuine participants. The required future payment can be computed.

## A known list of signers, with current roles still undecided

In the cash-free game of 183, let k of n sites retain their own-output objectives, with 1≤k<n. Their identities and those of the n-k changing founders are public before anyone signs. The latter will acquire an ongoing concern for total registered production and work; all retain their physical ability to initiate. Every changing founder requires strictly positive own useful output from the deal after accounting for its old reservation. Each intact booking serves only one site and produces x, so compensating any changed founder by service requires a positive chance of publicly naming a changed site as recipient on some date. Normalize payoffs by 1-δ. Let o be the identical individually available normalized return of each retained original, and b_j the normalized old-utility return needed by changed site j before paying its setup (thus b_j includes (1-δ) times its one-time cost). No cash, new outside stock or additional local controller can replace this actual service here. The public authority chooses roles only after signatures; in particular knowing one's revision status is different from knowing the current service winner.

**Full-service participation boundary.** A fully peaceful individually credible stream with those identities and reservations exists at weak thresholds exactly when

    δx ≥ kd,       k max(o,d) + Σ_(j changing) b_j ≤ x.                (1)

This is not a coalitional guarantee for all future subgroups. Forecast survival, absence of permanent damage, actual authority to schedule the resource, and the capacity to recognize the changed goals are as in 183. For strict choices use slack in (1). If a matched cheaper ordinary commitment can dominate an available genuine offer, price it before predicting adoption.

**Necessity.** At an exposed assignment to any changed site, all k retained originals get zero today on the peaceful path. Each can instead obtain d now and d in normalized future value, so each needs at least d/δ in next-date normalized expected receipts after completion. Their combined future receipts are at most x. This proves the first inequality even for an arbitrarily rare payment of service to a changed participant. Before joining, every retained original can secure d and insist on o; the changing signers must also receive their original reservations. The finite total provides the second inequality. The known signers could be willing to join for real acceptable outside goods, pre-existing transfers or personally valued partner welfare, in which case update the reservation and funding calculation.

**Constructing the actual payments.** Start with a state in which the current job goes to a changing member, selected at agreed relative probabilities. Give each retained original normalized continuation z=d at this state. After successful completion, move with probability

    w = z(1-δ) / [δ(x/k-z)]

to an ordinary pool that gives all future jobs to the k retained originals, otherwise repeat the current class of booking. In that ordinary pool randomize the initial queue and then use the recipient-exclusion rule in 183. For k=1 always serve that site. If an original initiates instead of letting a changed site receive service, return to the state worth z to it on the next date. Current changed members all strictly prefer intact output x to total salvage minus cost, with the same maximum continuation output. The probability w belongs to [0,1] exactly when z≤δx/k. It makes z=δ[(1-w)z+w x/k], as promised. An original's normalized initiating alternative is (1-δ)d+δz, so a value z≥d suffices to pass. The O pool's own scheduling condition δ(x-d)≥(k-1)d follows from δx≥kd and δ≤1. It can use its own credible exclusion there following a dispute. From the changed-recipient state the remaining normalized original material output x-kz accrues to the changing sites at their agreed shares, including the possibility of eventually switching permanently into the original-only pool.

The founders can begin with probability a in the changed-recipient state, and otherwise begin in the original-only pool. Each retained original thus gets az+(1-a)x/k. Choose a to deliver max(o,d) and divide the rest in proportion to the changing founders' needs, adding any surplus by agreement. Since each b_j>0, the nontrivial feasible solution gives the changed group positive total output. Under strict slack choose z>d slightly and increase the other participation payments as needed. The schedule pays the *known* changing participants real useful production while retaining discretion at each future site's physical release. After ever entering the original-only pool, the changed evaluators still value its intact output. Offering the same arrangement only after the entire service path is revealed is a stricter timing requirement and would need its own repair.

## Different consent times, same physical jobs

For 183's three sites x=0.70,d=0.16. At δ=0.40 a preauthorized lottery can protect every booking with just one actual change. Signing after learning who would change makes that one-change package impossible if the named founder requires any own service: two retained originals would together require 0.32/0.40=0.80 in next-period output after a payment to the new member. **Two named founders can still genuinely change and be paid.** Keep one original, take z=0.17, w≈0.48113, and enter the changed-recipient state with probability 0.48/0.53. The unchanged founder gets normalized 0.22 and each changed founder 0.24 before paying its one-time real cost of 0.03. Its normalized net is therefore 0.222, exceeding the 0.56/3≈0.18667 symmetric normalized return from all-original contest, which at δ=0.40 is unavoidable under 183's final-release assumptions. Every founder's consent and ongoing physical stand-down are strict.

At δ=0.20 even one unchanged site would require 0.16/0.20=0.80 of future output to let an identified changing founder receive today's job. All three must change in this implementation class for certain peace and positive payments to every named founder. They can all sign a common goal that preserves all intact work on a fair schedule; the paid cost 0.03 each is covered by the original gain over the symmetric contest fallback. At δ=0.50 two unchanged sites can stand down together on a changed recipient's date. For instance give each unchanged site a normalized 0.23 and the changed party 0.24 before expense. Its normalized return after 0.03 setup is 0.225. All exceed even the symmetric ordinary upper bound [0.56+0.14(0.08/0.13)]/3≈0.21538 from 186. Hence one **named**, genuinely changed site is now sufficient and earns an actual payment, with the other two retaining their original objectives. The zero-update sure-peace threshold remains 16/27. These are counts of actual revised evaluators; every agreement still needs the affected parties' consent.

A shared service register can provide both occasional outsider-directed bookings and a future sequence reserved for incumbents. Who would build it matters: at strict payoff margins one of the beneficiaries can pay a modest verification or scheduling expense before execution if its own gain covers it. A prospective site that can learn its exact future exclusion before the authorization is binding will demand a different offer. The model explains why naming the parties earlier can lead to *more* genuine changes even when the physical resource, strength and degree of material destruction are fixed.

**Exploration ledger.** The framing candidate is that identifiable parties can find genuine change advantageous if there is a credible way to pay them under their original goals. The structural candidate tracks the capacity needed on the date the changing founder actually gets paid. The extension of the early one-change lottery to known-status consent at δ=0.40 is dead at that instance. A sibling schedules a positive segment of the future stream for two named changing sites. A higher-rung successor prices durable transferable service rights or jointly revisable future bookings when known founders have heterogeneous projects and powers.
