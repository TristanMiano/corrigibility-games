# When the installer waits for a second chance

A genuine change can be valuable enough for every supplier to attempt it, yet too early to serve the very first batch. Waiting lets the original founder keep that batch and preserve the chance of earning later work. If an early failed update can be retried, many of those same founders instead pay to try immediately. The buyer can have a current-goal reason to fund a second installation socket.

## A reading with a date, and a scarce installation kit

Keep the four-lot factory in [241](241-when-a-buyer-will-lose-on-the-first-order-to-learn.md). Let M=3 denote the lots remaining after the first. The first lot powers a register read between production windows. In this version it reads the *then-active* controller. It cannot attest to the earlier date on a subsequently changed line. Parties agree explicitly which dates the goal is supposed to cover. A late-only bargain is genuine consent to a change for the last three lots. Calling it fulfillment of an earlier promise to serve all four would misstate its terms.

A can spend its one kit before the first job or after it and before the reading. It knows k, the same at either date. A late attempt while still original has expected value V₂(k)=max(0,q aM−k) over ending the relationship; failure leaves no access after the read. A genuine controller retains the agreed goal because it values each later productive exchange and a retained state would lose that access. A retained one gets X from the unseen first batch; imitating delivery cannot change the subsequent register.

With only one kit, an early attempt gives the original founder q a(1+M)+(1−q)X−k. Waiting earns X+V₂(k). Whenever late installation pays, the difference from moving it earlier is q(a−X)<0. When late installation does not pay, early installation is still less attractive. Thus an installer with discretion saves the kit for the later window. For q=1/4, a=2, X=4 and K=1.4, q aM=1.5>K. **Every founder honestly agrees to try at the later date and one in four succeeds.** B understands the first batch will certainly be kept and knowingly loses two on it. Its three later lots supply 3 in expected gains. After the .2 reading it earns .8. A earns 4+1.5−.7=4.8 on average. None of these losses requires a lie by either signer.

## Buying a real opportunity to retry

If each subject has a second socket and a fresh kit for a distinct attempt, a failed early installer can still make the same late attempt. Its current goal after a failure is still the incumbent one. The advantage to the original founder of *also* attempting early is now

    q[a(1+M)−X−V₂(k)]−k.                                         (242.1)

An early success acquires all the future orders without needing to pay for a second procedure. A failure still has the continuation V₂(k). In the example this advantage is .625−.75k. Founders with k<5/6 pay for an early attempt. All retained controllers then pay for the late one. Of all offers 25/168 have the agreed goal on the first batch and 81/224 have it on the remaining three. The second number is 25/168+(1−25/168)/4. Every positive retrospective read correctly identifies the goal at the later date. Its power to identify the goal *at the earlier date* depends on knowing this timing and cost selection.

| Available installation windows | Genuine on first lot | Genuine on last three | B's gain after the .2 reading | Original A's average gain |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Only before first exposure | 5/28 | 5/28 | 71/70 | 61/14 |
| One kit, either date | 0 | 1/4 | 4/5 | 24/5 |
| An early kit and an available retry | 25/168 | 81/224 | 849/280 | 16753/3360 |

B's gain for first-date probability p₁ and second-date probability p₂ is 6p₁−2+12p₂−.2. For the two-kit supplier, start with the wait-only gain and add E[max(.625−.75k,0)]=(25/42)(.625)/2. After first exposure the customer will still pay .2 to read the state in each design. With three lots left the value of information is 3 min(4p₂,2(1−p₂)), greater than the price in all three rows. Its choices after the read, both selves' service and possible return or contest on unused lots are the same as in 241. The absolute welfare amounts refer to incumbent goals; the successor decides how to use its own goods and the partner's goods under its changed criterion.

If the customer is offered the additional socket at .70, it buys it at the flexible one-kit plant: its gain is then about 2.332, well above .8. For every k the supplier can still wait and its option value is weakly positive. A fully funded .01 per site buys strict advance assent from the types who will never want an early attempt. A different plant able to install a reliable ordinary output router from the start could deliver all four lots at unchanged goals. At a quoted split cost of 3 to A and 13 to B, its original returns are 5 and 3, respectively, above both two-kit *mean* returns after the socket purchase. Suppliers who privately know k may rank the router differently; universal interim participation would need a typewise comparison. A router matching only the edit's reliability and the same installation constraints can reproduce its action incentive on these four jobs when bound to deliver after successful installation.

**Date-specific confidence.** After a positive at the two-kit plant, the probability A already had the goal on its first lot is (25/168)/(81/224)=100/243, despite a definitive current reading. Procurement of the additional hardware moves that earlier *conditional* confidence from zero at the flexible single-kit plant and changes the later genuine rate too. The comparison is about what is achieved on specified dates. It requires persistent working goals, returning failures that can use the second socket, enough material for the retry, and no right for the buyer to see the old state before the first operation. A preinstalled witness, a low-value calibration lot or immediately observable first outputs would let the parties negotiate other timings. Disposable commissioning components and a live status readout give reasons for the baseline opportunity and for the buyer to pay to change it.
