# When a buyer will lose on the first order to learn

The first batch can be a strictly losing bet even when the customer accepts it knowingly. Continued access to the same partner makes a successful change worth more to the original supplier, and lets the buyer use what it learns on later jobs. A limit on future access puts a sharp bound on both incentives, even with an ideal diagnostic.

## A bound over all ways of following up an experimental order

A controls a line, B controls the first real batch. Each initially values only its own goods. A can attempt a durable, negotiated goal change with probability of success q at privately known cost k distributed by F. A real change makes delivery optimal on every available batch. A retained controller strictly prefers keeping. A completed order earns the original supplier a>0 over the safe alternative if genuine and X>a if retained; B gains g>0 under G and loses h>0 under N. Those are expected increments conditional on current state and on the past. Production can still be risky. Each party can return an unused lot or initiate a finite-cost contest. In the application below safe return strictly beats the contest at every date. Information available to the customer is produced by an actual exposed batch. There is no advance proof or additional transfer in the class being bounded.

B can follow any feasible policy for later offers, based on timely observations and real rights to the stock. Let L_G^i and L_N^i be the expected discounted quantities released, conditional on each possible controller and measured with party i's discount. Count the first unit. If H_i is the entire expected discounted capacity accessible to this relationship, conditional on either state, then 1≤L_G^i,L_N^i≤H_i. Renewal opportunities and dates here are independent of which controller was installed. For a given policy whose physical responses are optimal for both live controllers,

    π = q F(q[a L_G^A − X L_N^A]),
    B's expected gain = πg L_G^B − (1−π)h L_N^B − c.               (241.1)

Extend F by zero below its support and one above it. These expressions follow by conditioning on whether the attempt succeeded, then adding expected per-batch gains only on histories where the batch is released. The same expressions apply to an adaptive policy: the decision to offer each lot is made before its fresh production uncertainty is resolved. If a retained controller can imitate a helpful action to improve its future admission, its actual optimal policy and payoffs must enter the comparison. Here a commissioning measurement can be informative independently of which action it chooses.

For common discounting and zero fees the buyer starts precisely when L_G/L_N exceeds (1−π)h/(πg); an original supplier of cost k attempts precisely when aL_G−XL_N>k/q. Learning must influence how much **future opportunity stays with this supplier** to change its founding incentive. If H_A≤X/a, even a costless perfect reading obtained after the first full lot gives the original founder no strict incentive for a positive-cost edit. The first retained order was already profitable by X.

More generally set π_max=q F(q[aH_A−X]). Every policy in this class has π≤π_max and gives B at most

    π_max g H_B − (1−π_max)h − c.                                 (241.2)

Increasing L_G and decreasing L_N raise both the original founder's attempt premium and B's conditional output. The bound is attained by a definitive reading of the active controller immediately after the first exposure, followed by every remaining order under G and none under N. Each continuation is strictly optimal for B and neither controller can alter the reading by pretending to deliver. At this procurement stage also check the actual cost of the reading and whether B would pay it after the first order. If the only diagnostic window is at commissioning, the most B would pay then instead of using the same unchanging prior on all later lots is (H_B−1) min(πg,(1−π)h), measured at the start with a same-date read. The no-offer assessment can also persist. Perfect information before the first lot, a smaller exploratory lot or a separate payment changes the bound's premises.

## Why four available orders can justify a loss on the first

Use a=2, X=4, g=4, h=2, q=1/4 and k uniform on [0,1.4]. B's safe return is two of its own units. Under a genuine controller an actual delivery yields B twelve with probability one half and zero otherwise, independently by lot. Retention gives B zero. The successor with criterion own goods plus half B's goods prefers delivery by one in expectation, and returning the unused stocks beats the equal-strength destructive contest for both controllers. Uncertainty about production survives even after the buyer knows the exact goal. The lots are launched in a short sequence and the products mature after all launch decisions. Releasing the first lot powers an otherwise inaccessible reading of the active controller at cost .2. Without that independent register the early delivery behavior alone would require a separate imitation check.

With four slots and no discount, the genuine fraction π=5/28 comes from a real attempt by 5/7 of original suppliers. Honest failures account for 15/28 of all offers and deliberate skipped attempts for 2/7. B knowingly loses **13/14** in expected goods on its first batch. Its three later orders, sent only to a genuinely changed partner, gain 15/7 in expectation before the meter costs .2. B finishes **71/70** ahead of holding the stocks. The incumbent supplier earns **61/14** over holding its stocks. B would strictly pay for the dated reading even after the first release; without it the prior 5/28 is too low to entrust any later lot. A losing debut, genuine voluntary changes by some founders, profitable deception by others and uncertain delivery even under a correct diagnosis coexist in this agreement.

If continuing to have both the line and the customer available has probability ρ at each transition, the expected capacity is H=1+ρ+ρ²+ρ³. Recompute the attempts: for 2<H≤4, π_max=(5/56)(H−2). A buyer strictly funds the .2 fee and the first full lot exactly when H>(15+√3089)/20≈3.52894, or **ρ>.91699**, in this definitive-read menu. The inequality follows by substituting into (241.2); below H=2 there is no private return to a costly attempt at all. Since the initial batch has negative expected value throughout this parameter range, an initially profitable agreement also gives B a strict reason to buy the reading once the first lot is sunk. This is a derived exposure threshold, not a claim that most partnerships last that long.

Suppose B can prebook the next operating windows, raising ρ from .90 to .95 at an extra upfront expense of .20. At .90 even the upper bound after the .2 read is −.176. At .95 the read and subsequent behavior earn .371 before the booking price. B buys the booking, A willingly enters and the genuine fraction is about .153 per initial offer. The reservation must actually preserve access to *this* line; an equally valuable option to buy from a fresh unrelated supplier would not give the incumbent the same continuation rent. A reliable ordinary router offered at a price each founder prefers can win these orders too.

**Naturality and limits.** A customer may be able to learn about a tooling setup only once a production run is under way. Several later lots can be authorized before the first goods finish curing. Whether that observation identifies a durable terminal goal, how strongly the opportunities stay with the same partner, and whether a cheaper nonproduction sample exists are stronger conditions. The general bound is useful even with noisy observations. A register that can reveal only the *current* state makes timing of the update an endogenous choice.
