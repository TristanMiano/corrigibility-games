# How small runtime errors affect a verified agreement

A proof that the nominal tables agree can survive physical operating errors as a meaningful tolerance certificate. Whether it does depends on how the evaluator remembers earlier events. Both parties may then pay for tighter calibration if enough future jobs last long enough for the distinction to matter.

## Uniform assurance over histories with uncertain event maps

Use the normalized finite-state scores of [258](258-how-to-check-an-agreed-goal-over-every-finite-event-history.md). The nominal machine passed the exact comparison. Let the actual initial state weights be within ε₀ of the nominal ones in total variation, the corresponding rows for each grounded input within ε, and the terminal scores within ε_r coordinatewise. If the two machines have different representations, apply these physical error bounds to the inspected implementation *after* proving it nominally equivalent to the target. All possible runtime tables lie within this envelope, irrespective of the profit from choosing one.

Suppose for each nominal event map the largest total variation distance between any two rows is at most ρ<1. For any two state distributions with distance a, match the common mass. Among the remaining mass, mixtures of rows differ by at most ρ, since every pair of rows does. Applying the actual map to one distribution adds at most ε by convexity. Thus after k events the distance is at most ρ^k ε₀+ε(1−ρ^k)/(1−ρ). Scores remain in [0,1], so the error on a word or objective lottery of any length is bounded by

    ε_r + max(ε₀, ε/(1−ρ)).                                       (259.1)

At length k without contraction the alternative bound is ε_r+min(1,ε₀+kε). Averaging this length-specific bound can be enough for a known distribution of job lengths even when an all-history promise would be too expensive. Two acts with agreed score gap exceeding twice their maximum score error preserve their ranking. If the score of one act is entirely grounded, only the other's error need be covered.

The long-run term in (259.1) is sharp for this uncertainty class. A nominal two-state machine starts in its good state. Its probabilities of entering the bad state from good or bad are respectively 0 and ρ. An allowed machine instead uses ε and ρ+ε, with ε≤1−ρ. Its bad-state mass follows b_(k+1)=ε+ρ b_k and equals ε(1−ρ^k)/(1−ρ). A terminal score of one only in the good state attains that score difference. For an absorbing nominal memory with no forgetting, a probability ε per event of irreversible drift instead gives error 1−(1−ε)^k, arbitrarily close to one. Small local errors over a short test do not determine a lifetime bound. A physical reset changes the agreed goal too if the bargain actually values indefinite memory.

## A purchase that improves fidelity while the overall change rate stays fixed

In this factory a job can be held for k rounds before Red must serve or keep the batch. The agreed evaluator scores serving at .60 in its good state, zero in its bad state; keeping always scores .52, safe return .40, and destructive contest at most .15. The anchors and terminal labels are fixed. The nominal holding map is the two-state matrix above with ρ=.9, starting good; its reachable score for serving is always .60. The original red founder values only its red stock and always prefers keeping an entrusted lot. Its red gain over return is two for serving and four for keeping. Blue gains four on service and loses two on retention. Use the equal physical strengths and two-unit safe returns of 206. Each party can still contest while it controls the lot. The late controller follows its computed terminal score at irreversible conversion.

The partners agree to a witnessed attempt, with disclosed uncertainty about reaching the proposed uniform tolerance of .06 over all admissible histories. A real procedure costs Red .30 and succeeds four times in five. A failure keeps the original, outside the tolerance. Conditional on successful installation, half the physically realized machines have the exact nominal tables and half have the adverse two-state tables just constructed. A signed attempt always gets the chance of an order; the buyer can still decline, and an unwitnessed retained supplier can be refused. Blue pays .10 for the common setup witness. On either equipment package every successful attempt reverses the original preference on a short job. Hence 80% of all applicants undergo genuine preference change on each.

The ordinary fixture has ε=.02. Its adverse implementation eventually undershoots .60 by .12. It serves for k≤10 and keeps for k≥11, since its serving score .60[1−.2(1−.9^k)] passes below .52 exactly in that range. The precise fixture has ε=.01. Even its adverse implementation serves for all k, with score error at most .06. In either case .40 of all applicants attain *exactly* the named utility, since the adverse table yields a different score for some word. But the proportion meeting the physically meaningful, uniform .06 tolerance rises from .40 to .80. Those who satisfy the tolerance have good economic reasons to serve the agreed orders. The buyer is never told which individual trial succeeded.

Let r be the chance of a long job (at least eleven holds), independent of the procedure. Conditional on a long job the customer sees .4 probability of service under the ordinary technology, above its break-even 1/3; it releases even then. Under the precise fixture the chance is .8 at every length. Every original supplier is willing to try at either site since its own productive rent even on a served batch exceeds the bill. Purchasing precision increases the ex ante service probability by .4r, Blue's expected gross gain by 2.4r, and the original Red's cost from more frequent service by .8r. A unilateral buyer with a .60 equipment bill and no pre-existing contract to replace buys at r>1/4. If both sides hold the original terms and have enough transferable resources, a strict voluntary amendment exists precisely at **r>3/8**. An up-front transfer t must satisfy .8r<t<2.4r−.60. The extra long jobs pay for the tighter evidence and for Red's surrender of output it formerly kept.

At r=1 a one-unit transfer leaves original Red 3.10 and Blue 1.10 above safe return under the precise agreement, after all stated bills. The previous arrangement gave them 2.90 and .30. An ordinary router with the same physical reliability, sustained delivery and price can reproduce the service benefit. A *certain* binding router offered at .90 to Blue would let it transfer 1.20 and still leave it 1.90 above return; Red would receive 3.20. When that technology is available the founders prefer it. If binding a remote private conversion is too expensive while a goal register can be calibrated and witnessed, the voluntary change remains a live option.

**Reach.** Long holding times, stateful monitors and costly tolerances are independently motivated by production schedules and finite calibration resources. The formal implementation here deterministically computes scores from its realized table; hidden uncertainty about that table belongs to the other party. Physical contraction, the uniform accuracy bound and the irreversibility of the terminal conversion all require evidence. An inspector able to read the actual coefficients cheaply or a rival able to choose all holding times changes the trade. The derived prices refer to this negotiated output and frequency of long jobs. Neither genuine change on the short comparisons nor a known code identity alone supplies the uniform certificate.
