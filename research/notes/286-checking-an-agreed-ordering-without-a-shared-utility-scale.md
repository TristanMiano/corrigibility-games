# 286 — Can two parties verify the same goal without agreeing on utility units?

A promise can specify an ordering over lotteries without specifying numbers for best and worst outcomes. One program could count a unit of value twice, add a positive starting score, or use an entirely different representation of state. A buyer who rejects all three as evidence of a changed goal can turn a measurement convention into a renegotiation cost. For the finite active event tables in [258](258-how-to-check-an-agreed-goal-over-every-finite-event-history.md), there is a complete check on the agreed domain that dispenses with common utility anchors.

## A proof or two lotteries the evaluators really rank differently

Let f(w) and g(w) be scores on the same physically grounded finite histories w; all finite lotteries over those histories are in the agreement. Either program may use its own rational output scale. Its finite table makes its scores bounded. For computations, each terminal column can be rescaled separately into [0,1] by any positive affine map without changing the question. Let the two tables have m and n internal states.

From the initial pair of state rows, expand both by the same events and keep one actual history for each linearly independent row, as in 258. Using a positive sign in both blocks makes no difference to independence. The row after every word has mass one in *each* block. Its linear expansion in the retained basis therefore has coefficients adding to one. Consequently the affine hull of all score pairs (f(w),g(w)) is exactly the affine hull of the pairs on the basis words. The nested reachable spaces stop growing by length m+n−2, so these basis words can all be kept within that length.

- If all pairs have the same coordinates, both controllers are indifferent across all lotteries on this domain. Positive scale one and the difference of their constants reproduce both. Indifference everywhere is behaviorally meaningful if additional reference outcomes are later appended.
- Otherwise find two different first coordinates. If none exist, f is indifferent between two basis words that g ranks strictly. If their second coordinates run in reverse order or tie, that pair already settles the disagreement.
- The two different first coordinates determine one candidate positive slope a and offset b for g=af+b. If every basis pair lies on this line, so does every reachable pair by affine closure. Taking expectations proves agreement on *all* lotteries, including countably supported ones by boundedness.
- If a third basis point lies off the line, sort the three by f. Equal first coordinates give a tie under f and a strict comparison under g. With distinct first coordinates, choose a lottery over the two outer points with the same f score as the middle point. Its g score differs by noncollinearity. This gives a **counterexample using at most three short histories**, even when both programs order all three pure outcomes alike.

This proves necessity as well as sufficiency without requiring prior knowledge of common external prizes. It also says what an observer can check if someone proposes a proof: verify the reached rows span the initial row and are closed under common events, then verify the positive affine relation on their terminal projections. A separate check of the pre-agreement controller can show that the founder started with a different ordering. An affine rescaling by an unchanged founder supplies no such evidence. With reference outcomes explicitly included in the contract, their positions have to be preserved too. In 258's sharp constant-versus-counter example, the two common anchors can be separate physical prizes outside the event table; a constant table alone cannot itself realize distinct zero and one *as event words*. The bound on reachable dimension holds on either reading.

For example, let three permissible job histories have f scores (2,3,6) and g scores (100,200,210). They order the three jobs identically. A lottery delivering the extreme high history with probability .30 and the low one otherwise has f score 3.2, which beats the middle job, but g score 133, below 200. A program returning 100+2f would instead preserve every preference on the same lots. The scale and offset in the proof describe what the two systems value over these choices; they need not be a line of code in either program.

## A domain with impossible histories

Sometimes the buyer is entitled to certify only histories admitted by a known finite-state rule. If its recognizer has indicator I(w), project reachable rows onto the three values **(I(w), I(w)f(w), I(w)g(w))**. Product each table with the recognizer to compute the projections, keeping all reachable rows during expansion. Discard zero projections only *after* closure. Accepted points have first coordinate one. If there is at least one, the same affine-line and three-word tests on their projections suffice. To see this, any linear relation vanishing on the projected basis vanishes on all rows, and the first coordinate of an accepted row makes the combination of accepted basis coefficients sum to one. A recognizer with no accepted histories yields only a vacuous check. With s recognizer states, s(m+n)−2 is a sufficient witness-length bound from the product construction.

Assigning both programs a zero score on every *impossible* word and then treating those zeroes as actual rewards can falsely force b=0. For instance f scores (1,2) on two admissible words and g scores (2,3) induce the same ordering there. Adding a new outcome valued zero by both would genuinely enlarge the domain and alter the attainable lotteries. This distinction matters if a proof from one contract is proposed for a different operating range.

## An incentive to purchase the less restrictive certificate

Use 258's batch market, with two permitted successful builds. One uses f and the other .25+.5f for the same nonconstant preference. A canonical-build stamp admits only the first. Actual terminal choices coincide in both successful installed builds, including comparisons with permissible lotteries. With the .05 stamp's selected working equilibrium only 16/35 of original offers achieve the agreed change and 8/35 are released. The scalable live table comparison priced at .20 induces all suppliers to pay for the procedure and .80 actually acquire the agreed ordering. At this latter prior Blue earns 3 over return after the check, versus 2.8 from blind release. Both devices serve the same production line. The inexpensive reader authenticates only the canonical build. The .20 device also obtains the live tables and protects their authority until conversion. Red's original founder pays for the change to secure work. A matched, cheaper ordinary device enforcing delivery can earn enough to win the business instead.

The structural premise is that both programs use expected scores over a shared set of consequential histories and the inspected rows control the coming decision. Independent units and different finite internal states are comparatively easy cases. States with unagreed meanings, private forecasts different from the objective lotteries, approximate coefficient access or a replacement after inspection are material uncertainties. If f varies by only Δ>0 between the two calibration words and both g measurements have possible error η, the inferred slope alone can err by up to 2η/Δ. A wider grounded pair or an economically meaningful tolerance can be worth buying. The exact rational proof does not turn approximate physical access into exact certainty.
