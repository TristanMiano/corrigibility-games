# Buying priority and sharing its returns

**E8, note 142.** The framing candidate is that a group can bargain over *which* shared goal to install. The structural candidate is insuring uncertain service with a certain payment. Here is an instance in the same rival-slot model, deliberately enlarging the symmetric division in 140. Judging the earlier symmetric equilibrium against this larger menu requires recomputing the outside offers.

## What safe transfers make feasible

For a coalition of m of three, suppose members can fund and irrevocably exchange safe output before the urgency draw. At a given allocation rule their z_i in (140.1) determine gross individual certainty equivalents F(z_i)=-log(1-z_i)/gamma above starting wealth. Funded safe transfers add one for one to CE. They cancel in the sum. Thus, relative to any individual reservations, the sum of CEs determines whether an allocation with strictly positive gains for everyone can be reached by transfers, when cash limits are slack. To construct it, compensate deficits first and divide any positive remainder. Check each payer's actual stock and transfer charges. If funds or enforcement bind, the sum remains an upper bound.

F is strictly increasing and strictly convex on the bounded service-increment domain. Giving one member a more certain chance and compensating the others can *increase* the sum of CEs. The nonlinear original utility is concave in final private output; the convexity here is in probability-weighted service increments. This distinction matters for a time slot that one can transfer intact but cannot split physically.

## The optimal priority rule in a class

Let every site's transformed urgency be either l or h as in 140 and let mu=E v. The right holder is independent, members are exchangeable, and a coalition can route only when its member holds the right, an event of probability a=m/3. Consider all measurable random allocations, with public types, among its members. Then 0<=z_i<=a mu. At a maximizer of sum F(z_i), the allocation must maximize the linear score with weights F'(z_i) at that point. Indeed convexity gives Fsum(y)>=Fsum(z)+grad Fsum(z).(y-z); optimality implies the last inner product is nonpositive for every feasible y. Compactness of this finite-state routing polytope supplies existence. A necessary linear-score condition need not by itself certify a global optimum.

If h(1-a mu)>l, every high site strictly outranks every low site under all these possible supporting weights. The marginal-weight ratio is bounded by 1/(1-a mu), proving the claim. Idle slots can always give someone strictly more. It remains to divide ties on all-low states and among the highest types. A fixed strict identity order, breaking every tie in favor of the earliest eligible site, maximizes sum F in this class. This ranking conclusion has a direct global proof:

1. Write b_k=P(first k members all low), b_0=1. Under fixed priority, the jth site receives z_j=a[h(b_(j-1)-b_j)+1{j=1}l b_m]. The entries are decreasing: the chance that one designated site is the first high falls as more preceding sites must be low, by exchangeability.
2. For any k specified members of any high-first rule, their total z cannot exceed a[h(1-b_k)+l b_m]. They capture a high-valued job only when one of them is high and can receive a low job only when the entire group is low. This bound holds for the k largest z_i of the competing rule. The first k priority entries attain it for every k; the grand totals agree. The priority vector therefore majorizes the competitor's vector.
3. For completeness, convex sums increase under this order. For every t, the sum of (z_i-t)_+ is the maximum over k of the k largest entries' sum minus k t. All these expressions are largest for the priority vector. Integrating against F''(t)>0 and adding the equal constant and linear terms proves the desired inequality. Strict inequality holds if the sorted vectors differ.

A negotiated common goal with increasing weights in the chosen priority, sufficiently close to equal that every high still beats every low, implements this allocation, with weights attached to normalized original service increments. On feasible outcomes a formerly self-regarding owner can strictly choose another member's high service. Each compensated signer's original objective evaluates its actual take including the payment. The same authority with a binding ordinary priority router can implement identical service; compare all-inclusive costs and private observation access. If payers can later reclaim the cash or renegotiate a new priority, recompute both the consent game and the currently installed goal's decision.

**Instance check.** For three iid half-high members with l=1/4 and h=3/4, a=1, mu=1/2 and h(1-a mu)>l. Equal allocation on ties gives each z=11/48. Fixed priority gives z=(13/32,3/16,3/32). The total remains 11/16. The resulting positive transfer budget over equal treatment is

    (1/gamma) log[(37/48)^3 / ((19/32)(13/16)(29/32))].

This is a gross budget before setup and actual transfer expense. At matched small positive expenses the priority beneficiary can pay the two lower-ranked agents enough to make all strictly better off. In particular the earlier symmetric ranking of coalition sizes must be recalculated if both a pair and a trio can bargain over these rules.

## How much of the priority result relies on ties?

Discrete high and low urgency model a job that meets or misses a service deadline. Exact equal utility within each class simplifies the theorem. To test it, vary each realized transformed value by at most eps in a common bounded interval with upper endpoint d<1. Keeping the same assignment changes each z by at most a eps and each CE by at most a eps/[gamma(1-d)]. Compare the fixed public category priority with the fixed random category tie rule. Any strict net CE-sum advantage exceeding 2m a eps/[gamma(1-d)] survives, including with continuous variation inside either category. This proves a robust beneficial alternative to the specified fair category rule. A process that always selects the largest *exact* continuous urgency, or lets parties file spurious priority claims, needs a fresh comparison. In particular tiny independent, identical continuous perturbations within categories give symmetric limiting mean shares under highest-exact-value routing; asymmetric perturbations can themselves induce priority.

Priority access and buy-ins have a plausible construction path. Members with cash, frequent competing deadlines and safe transferable claims can price a service priority against its risk. The beneficiary pays out of funded safe stock because its increased certainty equivalent covers the losers' reservations plus expense. The one-slot, CARA, public urgency and transfer assumptions specify where the exact price applies. Limited liquidity, verification, private urgency and a cheaper ordinary priority contract can favor another formation. Natural variation in who can pay and who holds authority motivates these boundaries. The argument establishes incentives where those capabilities are available. A volatile job reward cannot automatically be pledged as liquid collateral.

**Exploration record.** The instance reading “symmetric agents maximize their total transferable certainty equivalents by equal priority” is dead here. The lesson is to distinguish equal physical service value from insurance against uncertainty. A sibling negotiates deterministic priority with a funded rebate. A structural successor searches over the attainable vector of service lotteries and the timing of compensation. If liquidity is scarce, an additional sibling asks whether members can choose partial priority and a smaller rebate.
