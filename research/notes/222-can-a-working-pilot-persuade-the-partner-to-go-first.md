# Can a working pilot persuade the partner to go first?

A small pilot can make a risky arrangement worth signing, even though a working pilot leaves open both which goal is installed and whether the next delivery will succeed. The important distinction is between witnessing the *attempt* to change and inferring the *result* from performance. A performance-only pilot can also give an unchanged supplier an excellent reason to play along until the large order arrives.

## What a purely behavioral trial can buy

Suppose a unilateral edit is hidden. Keeping the incumbent goal preserves the same physical actions, information, computing resources and ability to randomize. Its owner is dynamically consistent over the job. Other parties respond to accessible histories; after conditioning on the supplier's actions the world has the same observation and output laws in both cases. Changing one's own hidden goal does not itself trigger a partner's installation or affect its private observations. Then strictly costly unilateral editing is dominated under the founder's current criterion throughout this agreement space. Fix the counterparties' strategies. The retained founder has a feasible history-dependent policy reproducing the edited controller's entire distribution of actions and observations, including a privately simulated installation outcome. Its optimizing continuation is at least as valuable under the incumbent goal as this imitation. It also saves the editing expense. The argument covers renegotiations, transfers, stochastic output and extra partners insofar as their responses depend only on such reproducible histories.

This claim compares *choosing* a hidden edit with retaining equal capabilities. A stable pre-existing class of helpful controllers can still be screened by pilots. The sufficient assumptions also specify where to look for an adoption repair: a directly witnessed procedure, a joint setup that causally changes what the other partner does, or a real difference in the cost or capability of producing the diagnostic behavior. A genuinely unobservable costly edit with a cheap emulating policy fails here; [219](219-which-uncertainties-can-more-experience-resolve.md) distinguishes the separate question of what observations would identify a controller in a restricted technological class.

## A witnessed attempt and a staged, uncertain order

A controls local processing. B holds a pilot parcel of size x and a later parcel of size L to which it can physically withhold access. Initial A values only its own final stock, B only its own. Both can witness the expenditure of an actual installation procedure for a meter cost C to B. Even the witness cannot tell whether it succeeded. Its known success probability is q. With no receipt B can keep its stock, and an unchanged A facing no future order keeps any batch. The witnessing technology must link the operation to the actual controller. A receipt for a nominal payment or a spare device would change the game.

Above the peaceful return, delivering earns A a units per parcel unit and retaining it earns X=a+d, d>0. B gains an expected g from delivery and loses h if A keeps. The changed goal gives the partner's stock sufficient weight that it strictly delivers on the final parcel and strictly prefers giving on the pilot, including the prospect of a later order. An actual delivery produces a visible positive with probability j, keeping produces one with probability e<j, independently across batches conditional on actions. A can observe its own goal. B is free to release L or return it after the pilot result. Positive probabilities, costs and subsequent orders are common knowledge. The truthful agreement asks A to attempt; nothing promises that each actuator or installation will succeed.

Consider a separating production continuation in which changed A delivers the pilot and retained A keeps it. If B releases the later parcel only on a positive, the old controller could instead make a real first delivery in order to obtain a later parcel to keep. It strictly keeps the first batch precisely when

    d x > (j−e) X L.                                              (222.1)

Suppose q<h/(g+h), so the full direct release following even a real attempt would lose B money. Set v=(1−q)h−qg>0. A pilot positive earns the later order when qjg>(1−q)eh. A negative warrants return when q(1−j)g<(1−q)(1−e)h. Under these responses, B's expected gain at founding is

    −v x + L[qjg−(1−q)eh] − C.                                   (222.2)

Thus the pilot must be *large enough* to make strategic imitation unprofitable and *small enough* for B to expose it. With divisible parcels, a nonempty strict interval exists exactly when the lower bound (j−e)XL/d is below the upper bound (L[qjg−(1−q)eh]−C)/v and within any physical cap. A particular parcel still has to lie in that interval. B's rational forecast after a trustable pilot depends on retained A's incentive as well as the noise of the apparatus. A more profitable future parcel for the incumbent raises the minimum credible pilot. B's retaining real control of that later parcel is part of the construction.

For a concrete job let a=2, X=4, d=2, g=4, h=2, q=.3, j=.3, e=.02, x=.7, L=1, C=.05. B owns lots of these two sizes, with the smaller one suitable for preliminary processing. Delivery can give B twenty units of final goods with probability .3 using its specialized finishing stocks and zero otherwise, compared with two on standalone return. Retention gives B zero; a residual meter indication occurs with probability .02. A earns two more units by retaining; a successor with own stock plus half the partner's stock strictly prefers delivery in expectation by one unit per parcel unit. The payoff uses expected terminal stock and leaves true output uncertain even with perfectly forecast physical dynamics. As in 206, returning preserves two units per side per parcel unit, and contest before conversion gives the winner at most three with equal victory chances after recovery and delay; both controllers prefer return or the specified continuation to contest.

Here direct release has expected loss .2 per unit. The pilot must exceed .56 units to deter mimicry, and (222.2) permits up to 1.41. The available .7-unit lot fits. B gains **.142** above holding both lots. Its genuine posterior after a positive is **45/52**; after a negative it is **15/64**. When it awards the later job on a positive it still knows that a genuinely helpful controller only succeeds in making the goods 30% of the time. The original supplier receives expected rent .3[2×.7+.3×2]+.7[4×.7+.02×4]=2.616 before the attempt expense. At k=.3 both parties strictly sign, B pays the witness, and 30% of installations succeed. For a failed attempt or a forged readiness declaration without a receipt the available options differ: a witnessed honest failure retains pilot access, while a completely hidden edit would be subject to the imitation argument above.

An unchanged supplier that can be bound on the *final receipt* can sometimes serve both parties better. For example, a funded escrow paying 7.2 per positive per unit gives an old supplier (j−e)7.2=2.016 extra expected pay for actual delivery, enough to beat the two-unit temptation. Serving both lots then gives B (1+.7)(4−.3×7.2)=3.128 over return before escrow expenses, and gives the original supplier 1.7(2+.3×7.2)=7.072. This dominates the staged edit if the payment can really be reserved and the relevant costs fit inside both margins. A plant that can hold its later parcel but lacks enough freely pledgeable stock to promise more than 2/(j−e) on a success lacks that simple strict ordinary repair. An equally reliable, locally binding delegate warrants its own matched price.

**Naturality and reach.** Customers often control physically separate trial and follow-up lots. A pilot may use scarce effort on a low-yield but high-value product; both its scale and its reliability affect the reason to imitate. Witnessing a real service visit can be much easier than reading its final effect. Ensuring it was applied to the controlling evaluator, and keeping a later parcel safe from seizure, are substantial feasibility premises. A powerful supplier that can reach the second stock immediately changes both the screening and consent inequalities. The sibling prices an outcome bonus against the available collateral. The broader question asks which irreproducible event can sustain voluntary change when ordinary behavior itself is cheap to emulate.
