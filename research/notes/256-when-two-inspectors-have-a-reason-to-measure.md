# When two inspectors have a reason to measure

Two favorable reports can give the customer strong evidence if the inspectors actually buy separate readings. Paying people to agree can support that outcome. The same payment can also reward reporting an irrelevant feature shared by both inspectors. The founders choosing whether to change their goals anticipate what the evidence will buy them.

## Paid agreement on the same job, compared with another job

Two inspectors each have their own physical instrument with sensitivity t and false-positive rate e, 0<e<t<1. Errors are independent conditional on the actual goal. The real job is otherwise the equal-strength factory of 206 with q=.8 and privately uniform installation expense k∈[0,1.4]. Let π be the genuine fraction induced by the release policy. Inspectors initially value only their own pay. Each can buy one reading for c_I or submit a report without it. In this first comparison they choose from their own information, with no sales income, private transfers or communication. Jobs in the reference pool are independently drawn from the same market. For every report the customer pays w plus b times the difference between two indicators: did the report match the other inspector on this job, and did it match a randomly paired report from a different job? The independent pairing is concealed until the reports are fixed. Base pay w≥b avoids imposing a loss on either inspector.

Let Δ_i be inspector i's probability of reporting positive after a positive reading less that probability after a negative. Thus Δ_i=1 means accurately reporting the instrument, Δ_i=−1 means reversing both signs, and an uninformative rule gives zero. For independent same-class readings the expected bonus above w is

    b K(π) Δ₁Δ₂,       K(π)=2π(1−π)(t−e)².                        (256.1)

Indeed the difference in match probabilities is twice the covariance of the reports. Their covariance is the covariance of their conditional means across the two actual controllers; within each controller it vanishes. Thus if my colleague measures and reports truthfully, my best choice is strictly to measure and report truthfully whenever bK(π)>c_I. Both switching signs gives the same bonus. If my colleague ignores the instrument, I save the cost by ignoring it too. Agreement can support genuine evidence while leaving alternative reporting conventions.

A costless, visible random lot marking can create a stronger competing incentive. If each reports the same fair binary marking on its job, unrelated to the goal and independent across jobs, the difference in agreement is 1−1/2=1/2. This is itself a best response against a peer who always reports the previously unseen marking, since no assignment can produce a match difference above one half against that fair report. Both can do better than honest costly measurement when K(π)<.5. If the customer expects these marks it learns nothing about the goal. A founder then gets no additional chance of productive access from choosing a costly change and gives up red goods if it does change and serve. Under this single ungrounded interface the no-productive-order continuation is credible. An actual witnessed attempt or an existing helpful supplier can give an uninformative message a different positive prior. [Correlated agreement and common inexpensive signals](../LITERATURE.md)

## What happens on the truthful assessment?

Set t=.9, e=.1. The customer can see both raw reports and retains control of its batch. Suppose it releases after at least one positive. The chance of release is .99 for a genuine controller and .19 for a retained one. The original founder gains q[2(.99)−4(.19)]=.976 from trying before its private expense. The actual installation fraction is then **488/875≈.5577** per job. Every cost type signs, since retaining still brings a chance of a profitable release. A single positive and a single negative have the same likelihood under either controller and leave the genuine probability at .5577, above the focal buyer's economic threshold of 1/3. Two negatives reduce it to .01533; two positives increase it to .99030. Thus the customer's proposed responses are strict. Its expected production margin is 2.04048 and blind release at the same prior would yield 1.34629.

At c_I=.04 and b=w=.20 the correlation premium per inspector is about .06315, above the expense of measuring. Both strictly participate. Paying both inspectors and .02 for allocating the samples costs about .5463 per real job. The customer pays even compared with blind release at the already-determined prior. The original supplier signs because it gets access to the work, the verifier acts because reporting what it saw pays at these beliefs, and the buyer accepts imperfect evidence because its positive releases pay for the occasional mistake. At equal strength both sides prefer the peaceful return on rejected orders. An ordinary controller fixing the final action can earn the same order on its offered terms; evidence from two peers on a batch does not itself provide a physical way to bind a delivery.

**Reach.** Independent laboratories and blinded cross-matching can supply the proposed information when they do not see each other's specimens or reports. Shared lot identifiers, a common apparatus failure, or private contact between assessors can supply correlations of quite different origins. This mechanism requires an identification of which sign and which physical artifact bear on the *agreed* preference. Its honest equilibrium alone cannot supply that interpretation.
