# 261 — Which joint goal can win both vetoes when a fault favors one side?

Two linear, exclusively interested founders choose the target θ in [260](260-when-founders-will-merge-under-an-uncertain-joint-goal.md) before surrendering their vetoes. They can disagree about what *should* be built while agreeing perfectly about a faulty compiler. Let q>0 be its chance of installing the negotiated interior goal and let r be the expected red share conditional on fault. Write aᴿ and aᴮ for the founders' continuation utilities in units of the joint productive capacity M, including any premium for keeping control. There are no upfront costs in the first calculation. With common beliefs their strict consent requires

    aᴿ < (1−q)r + qθ < 1−aᴮ.                                      (261.1)

As θ ranges over (0,1), the achievable expected red shares fill an open interval of length q. Thus a workable interior goal exists precisely when aᴿ+aᴮ<1, (1−q)r<1−aᴮ and (1−q)r+q>aᴿ. All three strict comparisons matter. A symmetric recipe can fail while an intentionally asymmetric genuine joint goal succeeds. When the fault already allocates a share inside the consent interval, both can accept however small positive q becomes. A high fallback on the side disfavored by the fault creates a positive lower bound on q. With different private forecasts, substitute each party's q and r in its own inequality and intersect the permitted θ intervals. Output transfer capacity, nonlinear utility and a separate cost of losing agency can change this interval.

## Power shifts the agreed weights before either founder changes

Separate lines return three red and four blue. On refusal either founder can force a physical contest that leaves nine of the twelve joint units recoverable. Blue wins with probability s, Red otherwise. All utilities in this example are linear in own output, with zero separate control premium. At s=5/9 both prefer contest to staying separate, so refusal gives Red four and Blue five in expectation. The compiler is faithful with q=.6 and a fault assigns every unit to Red. Blue would receive 3.6 under an intended even merger and veto it. At θ=.25 Red expects 6.6 red and Blue expects 5.4 blue. Both strictly prefer the revised goal. A successful implementation gives Red three and Blue nine. Red signs because the *full lottery* advances its original goal, even though it would prefer the red-only fault.

For s≥5/9 Blue continues to force contest on refusal. At θ=.25 its consent is strict for s<.60. Moving θ toward zero can still secure strict bilateral consent at .60≤s<.80. At s≥.80 even the most blue-favoring interior target cannot give Blue its physical outside return. Increasing q or adding a funded transfer can restore agreement. For example, at s=.82 the original q=.60, θ=.10 gives Blue 6.48 versus contest 7.38. If Red holds a transferable blue unit outside the shared equipment for which it has no other use or buyer, paying it on activation raises Blue to 7.48 while leaving Red at 5.52 versus 1.62. At q=.80, θ=.10 gives Blue 8.64 and Red 3.36 against 7.38 and 1.62 without drawing that store. Separate lines would keep four blue, so the prospect of recovering more by force matters here. In a setting with a cheaper enforceable ordinary split the parties can also adjust those shares.

## Would the red founder actually permit the intended procedure?

In the s=5/9, θ=.25 agreement, suppose Red can secretly bypass the compiler and guarantee a red-only successor at cost c. Given irrevocable admission to the joint line, bypass gains 5.4 in red output before this cost. At c=2 the founder would bypass on every job. Blue anticipating zero would refuse. Its veto prevents the tempting deviation from generating any production. Generalizing, reducing success by Δq gains Red Δq M(1−θ) in this all-red-fault setting.

A precommissioned, independently operated process monitor can record whether the physical setup was bypassed. An adverse receipt occurs with probability δₛ after a bypass and δₕ after honest preparation. Red deposits F red units in independent custody *before* receiving the lot. An adverse process receipt forfeits that external stock. The checker uses the already sealed process record and has no stake in the later color allocation. Red then honestly chooses the procedure when

    c + (δₛ−δₕ)F > Δq M(1−θ).                                    (261.2)

Red must also prefer 6.6−δₕF minus its setup fee to its four-unit outside return. Blue must prefer 5.4 minus its fees to five. With δₛ=.80, δₕ=.05, F=5, c=2, and a .20 monitor paid by Blue, all three comparisons are strict. Each original founder chooses the arrangement, about 60% of joint successors really have the agreed goal, and neither expects to physically fight. The five pledged units are a genuine balance-sheet requirement. Only the .25 expected forfeiture reduces Red's expected welfare under honest operation when reserve holding is free. A positive funding cost must come out of the participation surplus. With no process distinction or no credible custody the deposit loses force. A new outcome guarantee would need a separate analysis of how the successor can affect the trigger.

A lopsided fallback is plausible with unequal recovery equipment, while an abort path favoring one legacy controller is plausible when a pooled converter retains its incumbent default on a failed install. The same legacy owner has a measurable incentive to exploit that path. Funding an independently sealed commissioning record can win both vetoes when the order is valuable and custody is accessible. Cheap shared dispatch with two live vetoes can instead give each more than its quoted continuation. Equal physical distributions under a delegate can be equally valuable and count as ordinary delegation. Common beliefs about q, postactivation concentration of control, the red default and the cost and independence of the process record are consequential premises. Private parties cannot obtain its accuracy by writing it into the contract.
