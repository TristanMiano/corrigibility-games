# When better installers change the reason for doubt

Making an honest edit more reliable changes the supply of truthful installers. It also changes the customer's reason to pay for a test after the edit is over. These effects can offset each other. Keep the production, strength, private costs and available alternatives of [207](207-who-can-walk-away-from-a-doubtful-installation.md) fixed. Vary an announced installation success rate q. Separately vary the verifier's genuine sensitivity t, retained-controller false passage e and real checking cost c.

## Endogenous attempts, verification and confidence

For the public parameters giving an active inspection interval, write p_Q(q)=qF(q Z_Q), with F the cost distribution, Z_Q>0, and F continuously increasing on its interior support. The upper equilibrium branch first becomes available when p_Q reaches π_L. Within the active region its installation probability is

    p(q)=min(p_Q(q),π_U).

This follows from 207.1–3. Below the upper boundary, the same reliable test policy supports the change and p_Q is nondecreasing. With uniform costs and unsaturated adoption there are two identical proportional effects: an increase in q makes successful execution more likely and moves the private cost threshold in the same proportion. On the part where p_Q>π_U, B combines testing and untested trust. Its indifference pins p at π_U. The *intentions* supporting that same fraction of genuine goals vary with capability:

    honest attempt mass = π_U/q,
    honest failure mass = π_U(1−q)/q,
    intentional false claim mass = 1−π_U/q.                        (208.1)

Thus greater public reliability there strictly increases deliberate false claims and reduces innocent failures, while leaving the fraction actually installed constant. The likelihood of the displayed evidence depends on controller state and is the same for these two causes of mismatch. The same posterior about the goal can conceal a substantial change in the reason for uncertainty. A separate likelihood about effort or an enforceable prior commitment changes the equilibrium argument.

At s=.8 in 206, Z_Q=2.52, π_U=103/126 and the pure-test response has all costs attempting for q≥5/9. At q=.8 every installer tries, 80% succeed, and the positive signal yields confidence 36/37. Raising q past 103/126 changes the highest-installation branch. With q=.9 the installed fraction is approximately .81746 and the deliberate-false-claim fraction is approximately .09171. With q=1 it is approximately .18254. On this branch a customer who actually paid and saw a positive has genuine posterior 927/950. One who trusted without looking has posterior 103/126. Reporting just the confidence of checked, accepted batches would omit the unchecked acceptances. In the same physical game the lower-trade and zero-trade assessments have different conduct.

## Which side's capability matters?

For a fixed offer in the pure-test region, cheaper genuine installation increases F(q Z_Q). Better sensitivity t increases Z_Q by a−A_G per unit. More favorable evidence for retained controllers increases e and reduces Z_Q by a+d−A_N per unit. In the equal-fallback case the sign of a combined change with uniform unsaturated costs is the sign of the change in

    q² [ t(a−o_A) − e(a+d−o_A) ] / K.                            (208.2)

This permits a direct comparison of two capability packages, including a package that improves both commissioning and imitation. In the mixed-trust region put H=(1−e)h and J=(1−t)g. Increasing t or decreasing e strictly raises π_U=(H−c)/(H+J) when 0<c<H, g,h>0. Lowering c raises that ceiling too. A buyer's combat capability can change h, g, the installer's outside returns, and the post-refusal actions, as in 207. The formula needs those new physical payoffs before the effect of a power change can be signed.

For a *given* distribution of controller states, any more informative signal that can reproduce the old signal by randomization permits the buyer to reproduce its former decision rule at the old cost. That opportunity comparison is compatible with (208.1), which allows the distribution of controller states to respond. A supplier's capacity to deliver and an observer's capacity to recognize the goal are also separate. If a live goal is correctly installed with probability p and an independent actuator delivers with probability j when that goal instructs it, the chance of intended delivery via that goal is pj. Conversely, a funded ordinary controller can deliver while the original ranking is retained. Observations of delivered output have to be interpreted with both possibilities in the likelihood.

## What can the founders deliberately arrange?

When every attempt succeeds and 0<e<t<1, consider an attempted equilibrium with everyone honestly changing and a buyer paying a strictly positive inspection cost after signing. Both signals occur with positive probability on that path and both have genuine posterior one. If delivery by a genuine goal strictly beats refusal, B releases after either signal. It saves the test bill by releasing immediately. A capable installer then saves the edit expense and obtains its preferred retention after the batch is opened. These are successive best-response comparisons for an interface where the **sole** private use of the test is choosing release. At q<1 a test can be worth buying even on an all-honest path, because it identifies some real implementation failures.

A prepaid inspection by itself fixes only the spending decision. At q=1, if B can still release after either result and knows everyone complied, it wants to waive a refusal produced by a false negative. A genuinely binding physical release condition gives a different game. So does a repair that makes information valuable even to two honest partners. Each requires its actual cost and authority to be priced, and ordinary delegation can use available binding technology too. Improving q is a parameter comparison here; the buyer already has an established incentive to purchase ordinary tests in the ranges proved above. An installer's voluntary investment in higher q depends on who pays and which additional orders it can retain.

**Exploratory lesson.** Inferring the rate of dishonesty from a measured rate of genuine goals loses the distinction in (208.1). A sibling observes the attempt independently. The wider question asks which mistakes a partner can profitably repair or insure even when the probability of deliberate deception is small.
