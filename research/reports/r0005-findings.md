# When productive partners make changing goals worthwhile

**Fifth recurrence: renewable productive cooperation and voluntary partner
choice.** This round identifies a constructive mechanism for the project's
central hypothesis: agents can choose genuinely different goals because
those goals induce partners to create more of what the originals value.
The strongest result concerns goals that protect negotiated shares of **net
productive gains**. Once installed and correctly implemented, these goals
can make efficient investment, delivery and continued accommodation optimal
for both parties, including when later utility changes remain available.

The main conclusions are:

1. **A sufficiently large investment gain can pay for consensual changes to
   both agents' utilities.** A partner who expects an unfavorable division
   invests less. Negotiated net-gain goals can restore efficient investment;
   both originals benefit when the recovered surplus exceeds the actual
   costs of their changes and the available alternatives.
2. **Repeated productive opportunities and valuable willing partners expand
   the budget for a reusable change when they improve its relative return.**
   Better ordinary contracts or ordinary partner access can direct the same
   cooperative gains toward unchanged utilities instead.
3. **Affordable preparation, financing and appropriately defined concern
   support greater production.** Concern for net gains and concern for equal
   gross receipts can select different investment levels. A real funded
   advance can be worth arranging; writing down a future reimbursement does
   not itself finance preparation.
4. **Profitable coalition capture favors fighting; broader concern can
   support peace, with strategic responses included.** A group's concern
   for its own members can support attacks on outsiders. In a small example,
   compensating defeated attackers creates a combat incentive; stronger
   concern or a chosen safeguard can repair particular incentives, while
   cheap later threats expose a further safeguard boundary.

These are conditional mathematical results. Initial reliable installation,
beliefs about the installed goals and unrestricted coalition formation remain
open. The round supplies productive continuations, original consent regions,
dynamic choice results and specific repairs—not a claim that every capable
agent or every equilibrium adopts them. The [technical model E6](../models/E6.md)
and [claim ledger](../CLAIMS.md#r0005-renewable-productive-cooperation-and-voluntary-partners)
record the assumptions and proof locations.

## 1. Genuine changes can improve cooperation that is already peaceful

Consider an owner controlling a project's final output and a partner who
must prepare it first. Preparation consumes real parts and work. Once that
expense is sunk, the owner proposes a division; both retain the ability to
fight over the prepared project. Combat has a strength-dependent winner,
finite consumable costs and a smaller recoverable prize. The specified
ordinary bargaining outcome is peaceful: the owner leaves the partner
enough to accept rather than fight.

The problem is the earlier investment decision. The partner anticipates
receiving only part of the extra output its preparation creates. It therefore
invests less than would maximize their combined net gain. Changing the
owner's bargaining promise requires a credible reason for the owner to
honor it after preparation has been paid for.

The constructive package gives both agents new goals that value their own
net benefit and the least proportional net benefit among the agreed
participants. Their shares are negotiated; equality is one example. Costs
of preparation count before the surplus is divided. Under the stated
coefficients, each new goal is bounded above by its negotiated fraction of
the total net value. A physically attainable efficient, balanced outcome
reaches both bounds. Neither incumbent can improve its current goal by
choosing another division, attacking, stopping early or installing a later
goal that produces a different outcome within that same physical class.

This is a genuine preference change. After a large investment, the original
owner would prefer to keep more output. Its successor instead prefers the
negotiated net division. The original approves installing that successor
because the partner's larger investment raises its own eventual return.
The partner also approves its change under its original goal. No intrinsic
desire to be corrigible is needed for this particular incentive.

In the exact example, the comparison is:

| Quantity | Ordinary goals | Implemented negotiated net-gain goals |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Partner's preparation effort | 0.4 | 2 |
| Project output | 0.8 | 4 |
| Preparation expense | 0.08 | 2 |
| New installation expense | 0 | 0.1 per agent |
| Owner's original net benefit | 0.66 | 0.9 |
| Partner's original net benefit | 0.06 | 0.9 |
| Combat on the selected path | None | None |

The partner needs two units of input **before** receiving its larger return.
That funding requirement is part of the result. The increase is productive
cooperation beyond an already peaceful ordinary arrangement, rather than a
peace result obtained by omitting ordinary trade.

For output `a e`, preparation cost `c e²/2` and ordinary marginal share
`theta`, the total productive gain available to finance both changes is

    (a²/c)(1-theta)²/2.

Their combined setup expense must be smaller, and a negotiated share must
give each more than its actual alternatives. The mechanism extends to
increasing concave production and convex preparation costs, and to several
investors with funded net-surplus division. Exact zero-input complementarity
can leave a coordination problem; an efficient opportunity and its selection
are separate facts. [Proofs and example: note 110](../notes/110-investment-surplus-and-voluntary-productive-partners.md).

## 2. The goal's content matters, and later genuine revision can help

Equal gross receipts leave the investing partner bearing its preparation
expense out of its half. Sharing **net** gains first accounts for that
expense, giving the partner a stronger reason to invest. These goals can
therefore produce different outcomes even when both sound cooperative.

The distinction also supplies a further voluntary change. An owner already
valuing equal gross receipts can prefer a new net-gain goal because the
additional production improves its **existing** criterion. In the example,
the incumbent owner's score rises from 1 to 1.15 after the new expense,
and its partner's original net return rises from 0.5 to 0.65. The owner
evaluates this second revision with its current gross-receipt goal. The
analysis does not revert to the founder's original utility when convenient.

Conversely, giving the investor a strong preference for equal gross receipts
can make the lower investment its current maximum. That is a reason to
negotiate the content of the goal carefully. The net-gain construction is a
worked alternative; a richer productive opportunity or funded compensation
can also change what a current evaluator prefers.

The stability result concerns outcomes, not frozen programs. A later goal
that creates genuinely new surplus or adds a valuable partner can be worth
adopting. The earlier common-maximum bound must then be recomputed over the
expanded physical opportunities. [Further revision and goal definitions:
110, sections 7 and 10](../notes/110-investment-surplus-and-voluntary-productive-partners.md).

## 3. Renewal and partner choice change the return to a reusable goal

A setup cost can be paid once while productive opportunities recur. With
an actual improvement of `Delta` per project, adoption is worthwhile when
the discounted sum of those improvements exceeds setup cost. This comparison
uses the best available ordinary route, including ordinary contracts,
delegation, reputation and other partners.

Partner choice matters through both sides of that comparison. A new willing
partner can offer additional productive value, require a larger return,
provide a cheaper ordinary alternative, or require costly access and checking.
The useful condition is a higher **net return relative to the available
alternatives**, not simply a larger number of names in the market. The
round derives exact waiting, arrival and paid-access thresholds. One example
has an agent prefer to pay for access and then adopt at the first suitable
opportunity. [Renewal and search: 099/103](../notes/103-renewable-markets-and-the-option-to-wait.md).

Productive cooperation can also change the future opportunities themselves.
When it speeds access to a valuable future state, a genuine change can be
worthwhile even with no immediate profit difference. Improving ordinary
access reduces that relative advantage while raising ordinary welfare.
The transition law must describe actual equipment, access or qualification;
the favorable probability is not supplied by calling a route cooperative.
[Changed future opportunities: 111](../notes/111-cooperation-that-changes-future-opportunities.md).

The analysis also moves beyond identical replacement supplies. In a small
capital model, agents choose how much output to reinvest. More patience
raises both peaceful production and the winner's post-combat rebuilding
value. It favors a fixed offered change when the negotiated share exceeds
the agent's victory probability, and can make that offer lose consent when
the share is smaller. Renegotiating the shares repairs the displayed case.
Faster reconstruction likewise improves both frontiers and can reduce the
advantage of avoiding destruction. [Capital and joint parameter changes:
108](../notes/108-productive-capital-and-dynamic-balanced-goals.md).

## 4. What the parties can deliberately arrange

Natural occurrence and deliberate construction are different questions.
Specialized investment, repeated projects, finite recovery work and the
ability to choose partners are independently motivated features. Precise
goal formulas, recognized implementations and reliable accounting are
designed conditions. The important additional question is who can build
them and why that party would pay.

| Candidate arrangement | Actor, expense and current-goal reason | Evidence achieved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negotiate net-gain goals and shares | Both originals fund changes when their implemented returns exceed setup and their actual alternatives. | Exact consent interval and continued-incentive proof; initial staged recognition/activation remains conditional. |
| Advance preparation inputs | An owner supplies real inputs, recovers the principal from output and bears a real holding expense. Both installed parties gain from the resulting net surplus. | A feasible beneficial advance is solved, including funding and repayment; initial adoption still compares ordinary financing. |
| Search for or create access to a suitable partner | A chooser pays a search/access expense when its actual value gain exceeds that expense. | Exact conditional choice examples; counterpart willingness and the physical access process are required. |
| Add a productive member or reuse capacity across several partners | Participants can share additional net surplus, after entry and setup costs. | Coalition allocation and current-goal expansion certificates; actual formation and outside attacks are separate. |
| Choose a guard against exploitation | Defenders can benefit from a recognized rule changing post-combat compensation. | Direct-attack repair with an explicit cheap further-threat limit; stronger broad goals have a wider continuation guarantee. |

A particularly useful financing example raises net project value from 1.5
to 1.725 through an affordable advance. Each installed participant gains
0.1125 under equal net sharing. This is an action they can prefer to take,
not merely an assumed increase in the investor's wealth.

There are substantive ordinary successes too. Prepayment can restore trade
with one cooperative supplier. A funded shortfall bond can implement the
same service as a changed goal. Sufficient patience can support ordinary
reputation. A cheaper ordinary mechanism directs the gain toward unchanged
utility; equal performance and cost allow genuine adoption to remain an
optimal choice. [Controls and repairs: 095–105](../notes/101-funded-bonds-and-conditional-launch.md).

## 5. Which values and settings favor combat or compromise?

The round identifies several distinct sufficient conditions, rather than a
single temperament that explains every case.

- **Own-output originals favor ordinary peace** when a funded division gives
  each at least its actual combat return. They can still underinvest before
  that peaceful division, creating a reason to negotiate different goals.
- **Concern for negotiated net gains favors productive compromise** when an
  efficient proportional allocation is attainable. The installed criteria
  then prefer the shared productive maximum to feasible destructive or
  exploitative alternatives in the stated class.
- **A profitable capture opportunity favors combat for a coalition whose
  concern is confined to its members.** Strength pooling and the recoverable
  prize determine whether capture beats its peaceful share. More productive
  value that cannot be captured can restore a peaceful coalition allocation.
- **Broader concern can favor peace, with compensation responses included.**
  In a three-agent example, moderate concern makes defenders willing to
  compensate a defeated attacker. That insurance raises the attacker's
  current value of fighting. Higher concern restores direct restraint;
  the stronger common-maximum condition also blocks a profitable subsequent
  tribute-producing goal change.

The last example was tested with actual voluntary transfers after combat.
A proposed aggressor-conditioned safeguard deterred direct attack, but a
cheap further threatening goal could extract tribute. Both the repair and
its limit are retained. Getting an agent that already benefits from that
extortion to adopt a stronger stabilizing goal requires a new incentive,
such as additional production or compensation. [Coalition and safeguard
boundaries: 109](../notes/109-partner-coalitions-and-the-scope-of-concern.md).

These are results about declared preference and technology classes. The
quadratic investment example has broader concave/convex counterparts; the
coalition calculations cover parameter regions and whole declared coalition
families. Neither supplies a population distribution of natural agents.
The exact cost references, information and implementation technologies
remain central to judging how readily these arrangements could arise.

## 6. What this round establishes, and the next question

The main advance is a connected productive explanation: **a partner retains
influence through investment and partner choice; negotiated goals can improve
that investment; the resulting surplus can pay for the originals' genuine
changes; current-goal maxima can protect the productive continuation without
requiring permanent code retention.** Renewable opportunities, financing and
alternative partners determine the value of that package. Ordinary mechanisms
remain real alternatives throughout.

The strongest remaining gap is connecting these productive continuations to
actual initial recognition, staged installation and competing agreements.
A known genuine goal can support honest private-cost reporting; an original
choosing an unverifiable cost reference can prefer distortion. Likewise,
an allocation unblocked by productive partners can still face a hostile
outside coalition. These are concrete remaining links, rather than reasons
to discard the established consent and continuation results.

The next recommended recurrence is **endogenous productive-coalition formation
with outside combat and negotiated scope of concern**. It should construct
willing cooperative formations and equally test capture incentives and
repairs, using actual partner alternatives. This broadens the portfolio
beyond pairwise recognition games. Mergers, long-lived innovation and
population prevalence remain thin. **CG-03 and synthesis remain blocked.**
