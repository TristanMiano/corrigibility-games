# When agents favor combat or compromise

First recurrence findings and explanation. Updated 2026-10-31.

## Main conclusions

**Project-seeking agents prefer compromise when a credible division gives each more than its expected return from combat. They prefer attacking an offered continuation when it gives them less.** In the simplest model, agents value the amount built for their own projects. Combat produces a winner with all the surviving resources. A mutually preferred split exists when peaceful productive capacity, after paying for negotiation and execution, exceeds the sum of their expected combat returns. Strength determines those expectations and therefore which divisions they accept.

The first recurrence gives the following conditional answers to the project's three central comparisons:

1. **Values favoring compromise.** For agents that value their own project output linearly, a surplus over expected wartime output permits a split both strictly prefer. For agents with strictly diminishing returns to that output, a credible certain share equal to their mean wartime output supplies a benefit from avoiding risk. For agents that strongly dislike the opponent's projects, agreed restraint can be attractive: in the symmetric example, both prefer producing nothing to a contest that might greatly expand the opponent's project.
2. **Values favoring fighting.** A direct reward for combat raises the payoff a peaceful deal must beat; sufficiently large rewards put every feasible split below at least one agent's combat payoff. Increasing returns can also favor fighting over a certain share: with squared own-output utility, equal-strength agents each value a 50% chance of the whole resource at 0.5, versus 0.25 for a guaranteed half. That comparison favors a credible peaceful lottery too, if one is available. A strong penalty for changing goals favors keeping the current goal; its effect on fighting depends on the ordinary trade alternatives.
3. **Settings and incentives selecting combat or compromise.** Separate, protected control of resources sustains peace when each share beats combat. A revocable promise by a selfish sole controller instead leads to war in the solved final-decision game: the controller would take everything, so the excluded agent attacks. In the private-strength bargaining game, a proposer chooses an offer that sometimes causes war when the extra resources it retains outweigh the expected loss from rejection. When anticipated competition for strength consumes enough resources, attacking before that competition gives more project output than waiting for the eventual settlement.

These comparisons also sharpen the seed essay's proposal to change goals. **An agent prefers installing a compromise goal when the resulting credible cooperation serves its current goals better than its available alternatives, including the costs and risks of changing.** A proportional-sharing successor helps implement peace but also helps its opponent after winning a war, which raises the opponent's incentive to attack. In the equal-strength case studied here, that goal family supports strictly preferred late adoption only when combat leaves less than about 76.4% of peaceful output. Goals that respond differently to peaceful and wartime circumstances support adoption at smaller physical losses under their specified execution assumptions. Their complexity and sensitivity become part of the comparison.

**A moderate preference for preserving a successor's goal can make an initial compromise attractive by protecting it against later concessions.** In the staged example, that protection benefits the original agent; larger preservation penalties exclude useful updates. Over time, repeated sufficiently valuable opportunities induce voluntary change in the solved stopping model, while bounded opportunities support continued freezing. In the population example, the stated reproduction and inheritance rules sustain a mixture of frozen and revisable balanced-goal agents. Those conclusions depend on different mechanisms and are treated separately below.

The first recurrence supplies an initial set of preference classes, game mechanisms and parameter boundaries, mostly for two agents and divisible projects. The next research task is to establish their breadth across less tailored goals, uncertain environments and different bargaining institutions. **The project remains in CG-R001; CG-03 synthesis is blocked.**

## How to study naturalness

A useful starting case is an agent that wants more resources devoted to its own project and evaluates risks by expected project output. Its utility ranks histories solely by that output. This supplies a simple preference class to study; how representative it is remains a separate research question.

There are at least two separate naturalness questions. **How ordinary or specially constructed are the preferences?** Own-project utility is simple; a goal carefully tuned to produce one allocation before damage and another afterward is more tailored. **How ordinary or demanding is the environment?** Divisible goods, public knowledge of installed goals, a binding handover mechanism and a protected final production step are also assumptions. A simple goal in a restrictive game can produce an unusual outcome. An elegant compromise goal can depend on an implausibly capable installer.

Risk aversion, concern about relative power, attachment to one's identity, and reluctance to change goals should therefore be studied as separate dimensions. Calling all of them unnatural would prejudge the question. Direct enjoyment of combat is a useful contrasting case because physical waste need not count against it, but this round supplies no frequency estimate for that preference either. Establishing typicality would require a justified population or distribution of agents and environments, or a robustness result over a clearly described class. Neither follows from counting successful examples.

## The basic reason project seekers can prefer peace

Imagine two agents using a common stock of material to build different projects. Let **P** be the total project output available after a peaceful route and **W** the output the winner can produce after combat. Both quantities are measured after the route's physical costs. Let **s** be A's probability of winning; B wins with probability **1−s**. Initially, a winner builds only its own project.

If A receives **x** from a peaceful division, its alternative is an expected **sW** from combat. B's corresponding comparison is **P−x** against **(1−s)W**. Both strictly prefer the division when

$$sW<x<P-(1-s)W.$$

The interval has width **P−W**. Thus a mutually preferred division exists exactly when **P>W** in this benchmark.

For example, suppose peace can build 100 units and combat leaves capacity for 80. Equal-strength agents each expect 40 from war; a 50–50 division gives each 10 more. If A's victory probability instead rises to 80%, its war expectation becomes 64 and B's becomes 16. A can receive anything strictly between 64 and 84 while both still improve. Greater strength shifts the division of the gains; it does not eliminate their total size in this particular model.

Three questions remain distinct. Is there an outcome both prefer? Do their actual choices carry it out, including after an opportunity to attack or revise a promise? Does the bargaining process select that outcome? The inequality answers the first question. Much of this round concerned why the other two answers can differ.

If winning leaves different productive capacities for the two agents, replace W by winner-specific outputs **W_A** and **W_B**. A beneficial split then requires **P>sW_A+(1−s)W_B**. Insisting on a strength-proportional division is more demanding: it requires **P>max(W_A,W_B)**. A peaceful bargain can therefore exist while the proposed proportional bargain is rejected. [Bargaining and asymmetric recovery proofs](../notes/010-essay-formalization.md#6-physical-costs-unequal-survival-and-adoption-boundaries).

## Physical damage matters through what can still be built

The research replaces an unexplained war penalty with stocks, usable work, production capacity and time. Material scattered by combat can still exist while requiring collection, separation or reconstruction before it is useful. The relevant loss is the reduction in attainable projects within the available work and deadline.

For a simple example, start with 10 units of usable material and 12 units of work. Building a unit costs one work unit; recovering a dispersed unit costs one additional unit. With sufficient time, undamaged production can use all 10 material units. If six units are dispersed, only four remain immediately usable. Building eight units then needs four recovered units and uses all 12 work units. Output falls from 10 to 8. If 20 work units were available instead, the same dispersion would not reduce maximum output: all 10 units could be recovered as needed and built.

This explains two directions of change. Scarce work, tight deadlines and damaged infrastructure can enlarge the advantage of avoiding combat. Recovery capacity and spare resources can reduce that advantage. Peace has its own costs: communication, checking a proposed controller and installing it can consume enough work or time to reverse the output comparison. More physical destruction is not a universal argument that a particular negotiation process is worthwhile.

The round also corrected an actual modeling error. A slowed serial production line must spend time on recovery **and then** on production at its damaged rate. A separate throughput cap had understated that combined duration. The corrected model distinguishes serial processing from genuinely parallel machines. This matters because a mistaken estimate of W or P can put a proposed bargain on the wrong side of the participation boundary. [Physical derivations and correction](../notes/024-recovery-scheduling-and-infrastructure.md).

## Protected shares favor peace; revocable sole control favors attack

Consider one production machine. Strength determines who is selected to operate it, while the other agent retains the ability to attack. The operator can announce any split, but its final allocation remains revocable. If it still cares only about its own project, it prefers taking the entire peaceful output whenever the opponent is willing to let production proceed. Anticipating zero, the opponent attacks for a positive chance of winning the machine. In this final-decision game, every equilibrium produces war even though **P>W** and every ordinary split was available.

Now divide production into apparatus that each agent can operate independently. Each uses its share for its own project, and either can still initiate combat to seize the other's resources. With the same strength shares and total output budgets, the strength-proportional peaceful allocation gives both more than war when **P>W**. It is a strict equilibrium in the stated territorial control. The decisive difference is the ability to execute one's allocation without relying on the other's final generosity.

This comparison gives a concrete answer about simple agents: **the same project preferences support peace under protected separate control and combat under revocable sole control**. Total material, work and strength remain matched. Converting one architecture into the other may itself be costly. The result covers a finite production episode; later expropriation and indefinite territorial security require a longer game. [Control comparison and physical funding assumptions](../notes/030-control-rights-and-strength-mixtures.md).

Utility modification is another possible commitment mechanism. If the operator actually comes to value a specified split, carrying it out can become its preferred action. The original agent can willingly choose that change when the resulting output serves its original project better than the conflict it otherwise expects. It is the original goal that must approve the initial change; a new goal's satisfaction cannot justify its own installation retroactively.

In the main finite example, a sequential protocol supports one agent keeping its original goal while the other adopts a compromise goal. Their original-project outputs are three quarters and one quarter. A protected reciprocal change could give one half to each, but the agent receiving three quarters prefers the one-sided outcome. Sequential priority directs more of the cooperative gain to the agent keeping its goal. The unrefined game also has weak mutual-attack equilibria; a separate finite execution-error analysis supports the stated peaceful selection under its own assumptions. [Adoption example](../notes/010-essay-formalization.md#14-persistent-attack-can-make-unilateral-change-attractive-without-convergence); [selection analysis](../notes/032-finite-execution-and-equilibrium-selection.md).

A binding delegate with unchanged founder goals can reproduce the same physical continuation if it has the same contingent execution capability, costs and information. A revocable recommendation cannot. The unresolved comparison is therefore which mechanisms agents can actually build and trust at acceptable cost, rather than whether changing a utility has an intrinsic advantage over every other way to commit.

## A compromise goal can make the opponent more willing to attack

Suppose A installs a goal that always divides output between the two projects in the same proportions. This makes peaceful sharing credible. But if A wins a war, it still builds some of B's project. B now benefits both when it wins and when the newly generous A wins. Its incentive to attack rises relative to the original selfish-winner benchmark.

With equal strength, identical initial and later war budgets, and the proportional compromise goals studied here, a goal that A strictly prefers to install and B strictly prefers to accept exists exactly when war leaves **less than about 76.4% of peaceful output**. Installation is reliable and publicly observed; the selected goal must be bound in advance or changes must be visible early enough for B to respond. A 10% output loss from combat leaves too little room in that family: deterring B requires A to concede more than its original goal prefers. An ordinary enforceable split would still give both a gain at that loss rate.

That threshold is not a general limit on self-modification. A fixed goal that distinguishes peaceful from wartime outcomes can share peacefully while retaining selfish production after combat. Broader output-only goals can also choose different allocations at different resource budgets. Under the corresponding local implementation assumptions, these constructions recover the ordinary positive-surplus condition. [The generosity problem and threshold](../notes/011-contingent-goals-and-rollback.md); [broader output-only construction](../notes/014-smooth-goal-implementation.md).

The repair raises the naturalness question directly. A goal designed around two precisely known resource budgets may be less plausible than a simple proportional-sharing rule. This round established possibilities and obstructions; it did not establish which goals ordinary agents would discover, verify or cheaply install.

There is a measurable robustness tradeoff. Here an optimization error means losing at most a specified amount of the installed goal's **normalized value**, rather than a probability of software failure. As the physical surplus tends to zero at fixed strengths, the allowed error shrinks roughly with the fourth power of the surplus fraction for the studied quadratic goals, with its square for the broader concave class, and linearly when nonconcave goals are allowed. The latter improvement requires increasingly steep preferences; holding a normalized Lipschitz bound fixed restores a quadratic obstruction. These are mathematical accuracy comparisons; their computing and energy costs remain to be derived. [Accuracy results](../notes/017-approximation-and-goal-conditioning.md); [sharp concave bound](../notes/023-sharp-smooth-goal-robustness.md); [nonconcave bound and physical sensitivity](../notes/031-robustness-without-concavity.md).

Uncertain budgets create another obstacle. If the same physical output opportunities can occur after peace and after an A victory, one output-only goal cannot choose incompatible unique allocations on that identical feasible set. The stochastic model gives an exact boundary for when such overlap prevents a peaceful implementation. Learning the damage realization before deciding whether to attack can also undo an agreement that looked attractive beforehand. Thus knowing the probability law is insufficient; the timing of information and the goal's behavior in each realized state matter. [Stochastic budgets and information timing](../notes/028-stochastic-opportunity-overlap.md).

## Timing and bargaining can defeat a peaceful surplus

### Changing strength and investment

A split must survive the threats available when agents actually decide. If one agent is strongest early and the other becomes strongest later, promising each its strongest threat value may require more output than peace can produce. In the explicit path example, peace yields one unit throughout; the two agents' maximum dated combat expectations are 0.60 and 0.5625. No single unchanged-goal allocation can satisfy both, although a mutually acceptable allocation exists at every individual date. Transfers, a binding mechanism or a goal change alter this problem and must be evaluated separately. [Dated attack constraints](../notes/027-staged-implementation-certificates.md).

Even an enforceable final split can encourage wasteful investment in bargaining strength. In the two-agent investment example, one unit of productive work is initially available. Competing for a larger proportional share consumes 0.4 units, leaving each agent 0.3 under the later peace. Attacking before investment gives each an expected 0.4. Both still prefer the final peace to attacking **after** the investment is sunk. The incentive to fight comes from anticipating the strength competition, with no preference for violence in either utility. A credible restriction on deployment could preserve an earlier peace, but its enforcement is another mechanism to explain. [Investment and preemption](../notes/016-strength-investment-and-preemption.md).

Coalitions add a separate difficulty. With three equal agents, a linear strength contest and lower output after every coalition war admit a proportional allocation resistant to the stated coalition attacks. If combat effectiveness instead depends on squared coalition strength, a pair wins against the remaining agent with probability 0.8. At peaceful output one and war output 0.95, each pair demands at least 0.76 to match its threat. All three pair demands together require total output at least 1.14. No allocation of one unit can deter them all. This is a failure of coalition deterrence under enforceable alliances, not a proof that every coalition actually forms. [Coalition boundary](../notes/015-coalitional-combat.md).

### Private information and profitable risky offers

An agent uncertain about its opponent's strength can offer enough to satisfy the strongest possible opponent, or offer less and accept a risk of war. In the studied one-off offer game, the lower offer can maximize the proposer's expected output even when a peaceful offer acceptable to everyone exists.

For a concrete parameter boundary, suppose A's victory probability is uniformly distributed between **l** and **h**, known only to A, and B makes the binding offer. A pooled peaceful division is feasible when the surplus **P−W** is at least **W(h−l)/2**. B's optimal offer is fully peaceful when the surplus reaches **W(h−l)**; below that upper threshold it causes combat with probability **1−(P−W)/[W(h−l)]**, for positive surplus. Between the thresholds, avoiding every war would require B to leave more resources to weak opponents than it wants to concede. Wider uncertainty about strength therefore raises combat under the selected offer, while greater physical surplus lowers it. At exact indifference the specified response accepts the offer.

For example, let war produce 0.8 units, peace produce 1.1, and A's victory probability range uniformly from 25% to 75%. A credible split acceptable to every type exists. B nevertheless optimally offers A 0.5 units: types with victory probability above 62.5% attack, producing a 25% combat rate. Raising peaceful output to 1.2 makes B's optimal offer acceptable to every type. The change in the selected outcome comes from a larger reward for reaching agreement relative to the resources B saves by making a low offer.

A fallible public test can make a risky low offer profitable by making the opponent appear likely to be weak. In the solved binary-test example, a 90%-accurate test increases combat from zero to 8%: the proposer makes a lower offer after a low-strength signal, and the misclassified strong agents fight. Perfect revelation instead supports an accepted offer to each type and produces peace. These comparisons condition on the stated test menu and selected offers. [Information and offer incentives](../notes/021-private-strength-and-peaceful-screening.md).

### Detection and the incentive to verify

Suppose an honest proposal gives its maker 0.8 units, an undetected deceptive one gives 0.9375, and detected deception leads to rejection worth 0.75. Deterrence requires an effective detection probability of at least **11/15**, about 73.3%. If detection instead leads to cheap repair that restores the honest 0.8 payoff, any positive chance of escaping detection makes cheating profitable in this equal-preparation-cost comparison.

The post-detection alternative is therefore part of the incentive calculation. A threatened rejection is credible only if the agent would actually prefer it to the repairs and renegotiations still available. Verification also costs resources: an inspector who expects honesty may prefer to skip an expensive inspection. The round constructs both a mixed inspection equilibrium with some cheating and a no-trade equilibrium. It establishes conditions for those mechanisms, not a general claim that public utility knowledge makes deception disappear. [Deception, repair and inspection](../notes/013-deception-and-credible-inspection.md).

## Which preferences change the answer

**Reluctance to change goals** adds a cost to a modifying bargain, not automatically to an ordinary agreement. With linear project utilities, an update route must produce enough additional value to cover the agents' current change penalties. If an equally capable unchanged-goal split is available, those penalties do not apply to it. Hard preservation can therefore exclude modification while leaving peace attractive.

Freezing can also protect an agreement. In the staged merger example, A's newly compromising successor accepts a later redistribution that gives very little to A's original project. Original A anticipates this and rejects the first change. Giving the successor a moderate penalty for changing its goal again blocks the most exploitative later offer, making the initial deal attractive to A. A larger penalty can block useful renegotiation; a stronger enforceable transfer mechanism can bypass the goal-only protection. The benefit depends on the proposal and execution menu. [Staged merger and finite freezing](../notes/012-sequential-fusion.md).

**Risk preferences** change the comparison between a lottery and a certain division. With strictly diminishing returns to one's own output, a certain share equal to expected wartime output is strictly preferred to the nondegenerate combat lottery. With strictly increasing returns, that comparison favors the lottery. Credible peaceful randomization and conditional execution can implement the same output lottery; for these output-only preferences it has the same expected utility as combat. Giving each winner more productive resources then makes the peaceful lottery strictly better. The randomizer and execution costs must fit the peaceful resource budget. Threshold projects and indivisible goods are important extensions to classify in future work. [Risk and outcome distinctions](../notes/010-essay-formalization.md#3-ideal-war-deterministic-split-and-risk).

**Strong symmetric dislike of the opponent's projects favors mutual restraint when each fears the other's victory.** Consider agents that value their own output minus twice the opponent's output. With equal strength and one unit delivered to the winner, each values combat at −0.5. Peaceful nonproduction gives each zero, so both prefer restraint. If each also gets a 0.6 bonus from the combat event itself, each war value becomes 0.1. Every peaceful production lottery then falls below the combat payoff for at least one agent: the sum of their peaceful utilities is nonpositive. The contrast separates a preference against the opponent's output from a preference for the combat event. [Full-menu preference comparisons](../notes/026-preferences-and-the-full-peaceful-menu.md).

More generally, for own-project utilities with finite combat-event bonuses **b_A,b_B**, and a peaceful route with current modification penalties **κ_A,κ_B**, a strictly preferred split exists exactly when

$$P-W>b_A+b_B+\kappa_A+\kappa_B.$$

This formula uses the benchmark's common output-unit normalization. Physical transaction costs have already reduced P and must not be charged a second time. With several routes, compare all of them; an unchanged-goal route has zero modification penalties. This locates a genuine preference-based obstacle while preserving the distinction between rejecting a particular merger and rejecting every peaceful alternative.

## Valuable opportunities favor voluntary change; transmission rules govern prevalence

An incumbent with a finite freezing penalty can eventually change voluntarily if it keeps receiving sufficiently valuable opportunities. In the solved stopping model, opportunities arrive independently from a stationary, renewed supply; each offer is evaluated by its actual benefit under the incumbent's present utility. With a positive probability of benefits exceeding the penalty, finite expected benefit, and a discount factor strictly between zero and one, the incumbent uses a threshold rule and eventually encounters an acceptable offer with probability one. If every attainable gain is strictly below the penalty, keeping its goal is optimal indefinitely. Greater patience raises the acceptance threshold and can lengthen the wait. A closed finite work reserve can instead end the ability to modify, which is physical incapacity rather than a preference for freezing. [Voluntary stopping and resource limits](../notes/022-voluntary-freezing-and-opportunity-arrival.md).

The population calculation asks a different question. It specifies a selfish revisable type, a type with a finite freezing penalty, and a revisable type with a balanced goal. Physical project output produces offspring, and a stated rule determines which goal template they inherit. Under random matching and inheritance of the installed template, the example converges from the stated mixed initial populations to about **68.5% frozen types and 31.5% balanced types**; the selfish revisable template disappears. Revisability itself does not disappear, because the balanced type remains revisable.

This proportion describes the specified inheritance and encounter process. Changing inheritance changes the answer even when encounters have the same physical payoffs. Increasing the parameter for meeting one's own type produces different coexistence and extinction regions. Adding recurrent innovation makes every type present at positive innovation rates, though some remain rare. Utility, parent reproductive success and transmission of a particular goal are three distinct quantities. The population results identify explicit conditions for persistence, coexistence and extinction in this finite type menu. [Population assumptions and phase boundaries](../notes/019-selection-and-goal-transmission.md).

## A guide to the parameters

The rows below belong to different local models. They identify directions and boundaries to investigate; they are not independent knobs in one already-solved universal game.

| Parameter or feature | What favors compromise in the solved comparison | What can favor combat or block that compromise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Net physical surplus | P exceeds the relevant expected selfish-war output | Peaceful overhead erases that gain, or preferences do not value it |
| Recovery work and time | Combat damage makes rebuilding bind on a scarce resource | Abundant recovery resources make damage less consequential; expensive verification also consumes the surplus |
| Strength | A stable strength profile permits a division matching the threats | Asymmetric recovery or changing strength defeats a fixed proportional division; costly strength competition can cause preemption |
| Execution authority | Protected division, credible delegation or an incentive-compatible installed goal | A final controller can revoke a promise and take everything |
| Successor behavior after war | Sharing peacefully while retaining a credible wartime response | Unconditional generosity makes the opponent benefit from attacking even when it loses |
| Installation reliability | Under a fixed protocol, success is more likely | In the sequential example, reliability rising from 19% to 21% removes one contingent attempt and raises combat from 65.61% to 79% |
| Information and proposer power | Enough surplus to satisfy the strongest possible opponent; accurate evidence with credible execution | A proposer profits from screening; a fallible public signal changes offers and can raise conflict |
| Detection and repair | Detection is likely enough and its worse continuation is credible | Cheap repair removes the threatened loss; costly checking undermines the incentive to inspect |
| Goal-preservation penalty | Moderate successor freezing protects against some later concessions | High current penalties exclude an update; effects on ordinary unchanged-goal trade are different |
| Risk and relative preferences | Diminishing returns can support risk sharing; restraint can satisfy agents that dislike both projects | Increasing returns, incompatible valued outcomes or combat-event rewards can eliminate the offered bargain |
| Coalition technology | Linear additive-strength threats fit a proportional division when all coalition wars reduce output | Nonlinear combat effectiveness can make overlapping coalition demands jointly unaffordable |
| Execution accuracy and budget uncertainty | Enough surplus and error tolerance for the actual goal class and realized states | Narrow margins, overlapping feasible sets or late revelations invalidate the intended allocation |
| Future opportunities and transmission | Repeated worthwhile offers can induce change; some birth/matching rules favor revisable types | Bounded gains preserve freezing; other inheritance and matching rules sustain frozen types |

The reliability row is deliberately striking: its very low success rates are a diagnostic example, not a realistic calibration. The broader lesson is to distinguish improving a fixed mechanism from allowing agents to change which mechanism they use. [Reliability calculation and its selection assumptions](../notes/010-essay-formalization.md#16-reliable-installation-is-a-parameter-not-an-implication-of-self-knowledge).

## What remains to be learned about the project questions

The present evidence supports a conditional expectation: **when project seekers value their own output linearly and a credible, affordable division gives each more than its combat return, both prefer compromise.** Under revocable sole control, the excluded agent attacks; under the specified private-strength offer protocol, smaller surpluses and wider strength uncertainty increase combat; in the investment example, sufficiently costly strength competition makes early attack preferable to waiting. The next task is to establish how widely these mechanisms operate across preferences and environments.

| Main question | What the first recurrence contributes | What prevents a broad answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q01 Physical advantage | Resource and scheduling mechanisms that derive P and W | Recovery and verification technologies are stylized; their relevant ranges and joint uncertainty are not calibrated |
| Q02 Adoption and stability | Examples and boundaries for execution, information, timing, strength and coalitions | Most mechanisms are studied separately; representative repeated interactions and their combined incentives remain unresolved |
| Q03 Value of utility modification | A benefit over revocable promises, and equivalence under sufficiently capable binding delegation | The relative availability, cost and trustworthiness of those technologies must be derived rather than granted |
| Q04 Combat-favoring preferences | Full-menu examples separating ordinary project goals, freezing, risk, relative goals and combat bonuses | No justified measure of naturalness or coverage of a broad preference/environment population |
| Q05 Voluntary freezing | Conditions for persistent or eventually relaxed finite freezing, plus a strategic protection example | The opportunity supply and bargaining premium are not jointly derived from a general repeated game |
| Q06 Population prevalence | Explicit inheritance, matching and innovation counterexamples to universal disappearance | A finite public type menu and selected encounters do not establish resistance to arbitrary new agent types |

**The next research priority is an integrated conditional explanation**, as sharpened by the [2026-10-31 protocol revision](../RECURRENCE.md). Connect a defensible class of beneficial agreements to actual proposal, adoption, installation and continuation, or identify an obstruction that survives serious attempts to negotiate around it. Broader preference and environment comparisons should test that explanation. Treat agents' joint choice of accommodation and safeguards as a constructive mechanism; assess real alternatives while preserving voluntary preference change as a meaningful result when delegation can match it. Derive which conditions increase cooperation or combat and explain how natural or specially constructed they are. This direction leaves the next model open and starts no new recurrence.

The current results are a first installment toward that goal. They do not satisfy the gate for CG-03, and producing this explanation is part of finishing the first round's reporting, not moving the project into final synthesis.
