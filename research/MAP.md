# A map of the corrigibility games

*Evidence through the eighth recurrence, r0008.*

**We know most about what makes a particular bargain worth signing.** A founder can gain by agreeing to change what it values when that makes its report credible to a trading partner or gives another producer reason to invest. In some games we can follow the bargain from consent through installation and later chances to fight. The clearest gaps concern how often these conditions arise together and which goals persist as partners and technologies change.

## How much territory have we covered?

All six starting questions below have at least one worked example. Credible information and productive partnerships give us two materially different settings for studying adoption. At the population level, even an exact prediction depends on a particular rule for matching and inheritance. A percentage of *all* possible games would require a way to count and weight goals, institutions and technologies. We can track how far each answer reaches while leaving room for questions we have yet to recognize. [Original questions and evidence](CLAIMS.md)

## How would we know a partner actually changed?

There are three distinct questions a buyer might need answered. **Will this partner serve the batch? Does it now value what we agreed? Did our bargain cause that change?** A pilot can establish a useful level of service on familiar work. Checking the agreed ordering over new kinds of work also requires a reason why the tested choices carry over. Distinguishing a new change from an already compatible partner takes historical evidence. Learning what this bargain caused also takes an account of what the partner would otherwise have done. [Findings on trust and consent](reports/r0008-findings.md) · [Finding compatible partners or changing them](notes/243-is-the-buyer-finding-compatible-partners-or-changing-them.md)

The strongest positive verification result covers controllers with finite event tables and shared, grounded consequences for their inputs. Two programs can then be proved to rank every lottery over finite event histories the same way even when they use different numerical units. On a finite production menu, a limit on the material cost of a mistaken ranking can be enough for both founders to sign. With an unknown actuator, an unobserved change of controller or new meanings for a product, those premises need fresh evidence. Two laboratories can agree on an irrelevant public label. Separate, independently checked samples can give them a reason to measure the requested property. [Checking lottery rankings](notes/286-checking-an-agreed-ordering-without-a-shared-utility-scale.md) · [Pricing partial agreement](notes/287-how-much-a-buyer-can-lose-when-a-goal-is-only-approximately-right.md) · [Two interested inspectors](notes/257-when-two-certificates-are-worth-more-than-one.md)

The same evidence can have different uses at different stakes. In one market, relaxing the confidence required of accepted suppliers from certainty to about 92% lets every genuinely changed supplier work while rejected suppliers remain safe. In another, two founders willingly attempt to change when insurance protects their own output and the chance both succeed is bounded only between 56% and 75%. A second uncertainty concerns who gets what under two outcomes the common goal ranks equally. One plant pays to make its shared production criterion favor the more productive recipe, and also compensates the partner whose consent it needs. These are worked boundaries for named games. [What the common goal protects](notes/288-when-agreement-on-a-common-goal-protects-both-founders.md) · [Certifying a partner and protecting a refusal](notes/282-how-much-certainty-can-a-peaceful-market-afford.md) · [Signing across uncertain forecasts](notes/270-how-certain-must-the-partners-be-about-joint-success.md)

## Why would a blue paperclip maximizer choose to care about red?

Suppose research at either factory helps both, but each pays for its own research. In one example, choosing research independently gives each founder 21.5 clips of its own color. If both reliably adopt a goal that values both colors, each does more research. After paying for the changes, each gets 21.875 clips of the only color it originally valued. The founders have a reason to sign under their old goals, and their successors have a reason to do the work under their new ones.

What matters is *what the factories share*. A reusable design can benefit both. A cheaper, equally capable ordinary contract can secure the same work while both founders keep their goals. A machine that one factory can seize creates a different incentive. When recovery leaves enough useful equipment and settlement is expensive, seizing it can pay. [Paperclip findings](reports/r0006-findings.md)

## Which conditions might we actually encounter?

Shared designs, scarce equipment and queues for future access have independent reasons to arise. Chandra's observing awards, for example, include projects granted time at several facilities. This grounds the scheduling problem. The prices of reliably changing a goal or binding a local decision still need separate evidence. Our most detailed results concentrate on measurable projects, identifiable participants and a few specified ways of making a promise credible. [Chandra joint awards](https://cxc.harvard.edu/target_lists/cycle26/cycle26_approved_joint.html) · [Institutional comparisons and their limits](reports/r0007-findings.md#how-widely-should-these-conclusions-travel)

**Who would pay to create those conditions?** In a three-plant circuit, one plant will fund a missing adapter if it can secure enough of the resulting services before it buys. If it must buy first and bargain later, its expected share falls below the price. In another model a founder pays to postpone naming the recipient of a batch, after securing a larger share of the savings. That purchase keeps every job peaceful while cutting actual goal installations from 1.20 to 0.30 per job in the example. [Funding the adapter](notes/168-what-a-builder-can-retain-after-renegotiation.md) · [Paying for later assignment](notes/194-how-late-assignment-changes-the-price-of-accommodation.md)

## Reading the frontier

**What moved.** The first round explored splitting control, merging agents, deceptive offers and a founder changing its goal unilaterally. Rounds two through four followed protected partnerships and the price of credible information. Rounds five through seven added renewable investment, factories with distinct final goods, and coalitions sharing scarce work. [Earlier scope review](reports/2026-11-03-scope-and-incentives-review.md) · [Run index and findings](runs/README.md)

The seventh round priced the consent of named coalition members. The eighth followed a promised utility change through a fallible installation, its inspection, delivery and a possible dispute. An offer of training can make a failed pilot worth accepting. A buyer can even pay to delay seeing the goal reading when its ability to reject unlucky attempts would otherwise deter the very change it seeks. [Named signers](reports/r0007-findings.md#who-would-actually-change) · [What a failed pilot means](notes/245-when-helping-after-a-failed-pilot-makes-success-more-informative.md) · [Paying for the timing of evidence](notes/264-when-buyers-can-recheck-whichever-receipts-they-doubt.md)

**The nearest named gap** remains a market with repeated entry by suppliers of equipment or ordinary controllers (R766). We now know how renewals can change the working mix when applicants arrive under a specified rule. Do suppliers pay to enter repeatedly, which founders change when those offers arrive, and which goals survive new bargains? Competing custodians who sell credentials as well as equipment are one way to connect that question to the eighth round. [Finite entry and actual bidders](notes/249-will-another-buyer-actually-show-up.md) · [Open questions and tests](runs/r0008.md#frontier)

Even knowing the separate distributions of skill and power would leave substantial uncertainty about prevalence. In one three-site example, assigning the same skills to the same strengths in different combinations gives anywhere from one third to all sites some profitable verified trade. Measuring which conditions occur *together*, and which feasible agreement a market actually selects, would narrow that range. The parties can sometimes change the association themselves. In a two-job example they pay to put reliable kits on the batches where a mistake costs nine units, preserving the aggregate 75% installation rate while eliminating conflict. [A chosen reassignment](notes/289-when-it-pays-to-move-reliable-installers-to-high-stakes-jobs.md) [What separate surveys can tell us](notes/273-what-skill-and-strength-surveys-can-say-about-a-population.md)

**The outer edge is harder to see.** One way to look for missing territory is to hold output fixed and ask what a founder could still care about. Would it sign the same bargain if its descendants got the same resources but lost the right to choose their partners? This is a tentative lead at the intersection of preferences about successors and control over future encounters. Further work might split an existing question or reveal one that belongs on a different row altogether. [Long-run questions in the charter](CHARTER.md#central-distinctions)
